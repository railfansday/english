#1. Nuclear radiation in Russia, Ukraine? No, just hacked warning alerts

Warnings startle people in Ukraine, but authorities say they cleared up the confusion in under an hour.

Ukraine and Russia have accused each other of hacking into systems used to send out emergency alerts to citizens, raising fears of a new escalation of digital and psychological warfare tactics.

The systems’ apps sent out radiation alerts on Sunday and Monday to civilians before officials issued public clarifications.

The fake threats began on the same day as a localised ceasefire in the Russian-occupied Kherson region came to an end on Sunday. The truce, which the United States said was brokered by first lady Melania Trump, is the longest so far in Russia’s more than four-year war on Ukraine. The ceasefire lasted for six days, during which humanitarian aid and evacuations were allowed in the Russian-occupied town of Olekshy.

Al Jazeera’s Audrey Macalpine, reporting from Kyiv, said many people in Ukraine now expect renewed fighting along the front lines.

Here’s what we know about the fake notifications:

Ukraine’s Air Force said late on Sunday that users of its Air Alert app, which warns people of incoming air attacks, received fake warnings of nuclear and chemical threats to areas in and around Kyiv.

Users were also warned of potential street combat and told that Russia’s hypersonic Oreshnik missile had been deployed.

“The air alert app appears to have been subjected to enemy influence,” the Ukrainian Air Force said on Telegram.

“There is no radiation threat! Stay calm and follow our updates on our channel.”

Ukraine’s Center for Strategic Communications blamed Russia. In a post on X, the agency pointed to the use of the Russian language in one of the fake messages. The Ukrainian government said a separate app created for the capital city was working normally.

The fake alerts came just after 10pm (20:00 GMT), startling a lot of people, Macalpine reported.

“Even the speakers here in the centre of the city rang out warnings of these attacks,” she said. “But most people here understood within at least half an hour that this was some kind of hacking attack.”

Hours later on Monday morning, similar announcements came from Russia’s Ministry of Civil Defence, Emergencies and Disaster Relief, which said its warnings app came under a “massive cyberattack” and wrongly warned users of imminent drone attacks. The ministry did not name any suspects but said the alerts spread “false information”.

Media reports said the hackers warned Russians that “nuclear warheads mounted on P1-Suns [Ukrainian drones] had been launched on all regions” of the country. The alerts also instructed people to “urgently” stock up on food for three weeks and to hide.

This appears to be the first time either Ukraine’s or Russia’s official alert apps have been hacked.

However, cyberwarfare has long been a major part of the countries’ previous confrontations.

Russian hackers have attacked Ukraine’s election systems in the past and in January 2022 attacked Ukrainian government and banking sites. Cyberwarfare tactics have only intensified since February 2022 when Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. In late 2023, the Russian hacker group Sandworm took the private telecommunications network Kyivstar offline, disrupting notifications for days.

Ukrainian groups have also launched multiple cyberattacks on Russia.

The country’s volunteer-based IT Army of Ukraine was founded in the wake of Russia’s invasion specifically to engage in cyberwarfare against Russia and has reportedly targeted the Moscow Stock Exchange, Russia’s power grids and several government websites. In 2022, the Reuters news agency reported Ukrainian attackers used facial recognition software to scan the faces of dead Russian combatants and notified their families of their deaths.

Ukraine is particularly vulnerable because it has already suffered one nuclear disaster and it remains at risk due to fighting around another major nuclear plant.

In April 1986, the Chornobyl nuclear power plant, located in the then-Ukrainian Soviet Republic, exploded, releasing radioactive material. Scores of people died from the explosion itself, but thousands later developed severe health issues as a result, including thyroid cancer. Radiation was detected as far away as Sweden. It’s regarded as one of the two worst nuclear accidents in history along with the 2011 Fukushima disaster in Japan. Chornobyl remains a ghost town after its inhabitants were evacuated.

Ongoing fighting in the Ukrainian region of Zaporizhia has raised fears that the tragic story could repeat itself. Russia partly controls the region, including Enerhodar, where the Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, Europe’s largest nuclear energy facility, is located.

Both sides have repeatedly launched attacks on each other’s positions with Russia targeting the Ukrainian-held provincial capital, Zaporizhzhia, and Ukraine targeting Russian-held areas, including Enerhodar.

Last week, the International Atomic Energy Agency, the United Nations nuclear watchdog, said a welder working at the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant was killed in a drone attack. The head of the agency, Rafael Grossi, called for “maximum military restraint” from both sides.

#2. Mother told to book appointment to give birth to her dead baby

Anthony and Danielle now have tattoos of Maisie's hand and footprint

Warning: This article contains themes and images some readers may find upsetting.

A pregnant woman whose baby had died was sent home from hospital still carrying the child and told to make an appointment later in the week to give birth.

Danielle Brown, 31, from North Tyneside, had already been wrongly discharged despite having the life-threatening condition pre-eclampsia and had returned because she was still unwell.

After insisting on returning to the maternity unit, she was finally seen and doctors discovered her baby had died. Danielle would later describe the silence, the screen turned away from her, and turning to her partner to say: "I think she's gone."

Being sent home with Danielle carrying the baby was "horrible", her partner said, "lying in bed and holding that belly" knowing their baby was not alive.

The Newcastle Hospitals NHS Trust said it fully accepted "aspects of the care we provided fell short" and offered a "heartfelt apology".

Danielle and her 34-year-old partner Anthony Miller had been delighted to learn they were having a baby girl.

"I was just over the moon, I couldn't be happier," Anthony said.

They decided to call their daughter Maisie.

But, seven months into her pregnancy in February 2023, Danielle woke up feeling unwell. Her face was swollen, as were her hands and feet - all potential signs of pre-eclampsia, external, which can be life threatening to both mother and baby.

Anthony and Danielle were overjoyed to learn they were having a girl

The couple went to the Northumbria Specialist Emergency Care Hospital in Cramlington, Northumberland, but medics found problems with Maisie's heart rate and told Danielle her baby needed to be delivered immediately via emergency caesarean section, external.

"Maisie's heart rate had dropped and a midwife ran in and said 'don't panic, but I need to pull the emergency cord - you're having your baby'," Danielle said.

The midwife told her space was being found for her at the Royal Victoria Infirmary (RVI) in Newcastle and doctors there "were going to do the section", she said.

She was transferred by ambulance but, once in Newcastle, Danielle did not have the operation and was instead put on a ward.

"A consultant came to see me and he said I am staying in hospital until Maisie was born," she said.

But, the following day, another doctor reviewed her and sent her home.

Within 24 hours, again feeling unwell, Danielle called the maternity unit and said she was told she probably had a stomach bug. But she insisted on being seen.

"When I finally had a scan, the screen was turned away and there was no sound," Danielle said. She remembers turning to her partner and saying: "I think she's gone."

Doctors were unable to find Maisie's heartbeat and they told Danielle her baby had died.

But, instead of delivering the baby, as she expected, Danielle was given a phone number, and sent home. She said they told her to "give us a call on Friday morning, and book a slot to come in and have Maisie".

"It was horrible," Anthony said. "Lying in bed and holding that belly, and still being able to feel Maisie but knowing she wasn't alive."

Danielle and her partner Anthony named their baby Maisie

Two days later and after 10 hours in labour, Danielle gave birth to a baby girl.

"When Maisie was born the room was silent," she said. "Seeing Maisie was bittersweet. She was perfect. I told her she was beautiful and I loved her.

"It was devastating that my first experience of holding a baby was holding my stillborn daughter."

Speaking publicly for the first time about what happened, Danielle said no-one should be sent home still carrying a baby who has died.

"Don't," she said. "Just take that family and help them. Help them.

"When we left hospital, we gave Maisie one last cuddle and kiss and told her we loved her and were sorry things couldn't have been different."

But, after saying goodbye to their daughter, the couple faced further distress. To leave the hospital they had to walk past other parents holding their babies.

"We should have been leaving with our baby too, but we were leaving with a memory box," Danielle said. "All the new mums were walking out carrying their babies.

The couple want every hospital in the UK to provide an alternative way out for grieving mothers, to "make a different route for a parent who has just lost a child".

Danielle Brown and Anthony Miller had been looking forward to the birth of their baby girl

The trust's executive director of nursing and midwifery, Ian Joy, said: "We fully accept that aspects of the care we provided fell short of the standards the family had every right to expect and offer a heartfelt apology for this.

"Following a detailed review in 2023, we improved urgent assessment for women who come to our maternity services with unexpected symptoms or suspected complications and introduced quicker escalation procedures when there are concerns."

"The trust admitted Danielle should not have been discharged on either the 14 or 15 of February 2023. Had she remained in hospital, monitoring would have shown the need to speed up delivery and Maisie would have been born alive."

"Our deepest sympathies are with the family for their loss and the distress caused.

"These failings in care are unacceptable and should never have happened," they said.

#3. US midterms: 1 day to go – Democrats rally around El-Sayed amid attacks

Democrats slam Haley Stevens over her endorsement of a Republican candidate a day before the polls.

With one day to go before the United States midterm elections, campaigning has reached a fever pitch with Democratic and Republican leaders pulling out all the stops to mobilise voters.

Democrats are hoping to flip control of both chambers of Congress, which is currently controlled by US President Donald Trump’s Republican Party.

All 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 of the Senate’s 100 seats are up for grabs on Tuesday. Voting will also be held to elect governors in 36 of the 50 US states.

With the cost-of-living crisis, exacerbated by the US-Israel war on Iran, becoming one of the key talking points of these elections, here’s where things stand as of Monday:

In a dramatic political development on Sunday, Democratic Congresswoman Haley Stevens threw her support behind Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers over her own party’s nominee, Abdul El-Sayed, in Michigan – one of the most closely watched races of the US midterm elections. Stevens had lost Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary in August to El-Sayed.

Her announcement followed weeks of tension with El-Sayed over US support to Israel, which has been accused of committing genocide in Gaza. El-Sayed has opposed military support to Israel, arguing that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu pushed the US into the Iran war. Stevens has accused El-Sayed of anti-Semitism for his criticism of Israeli actions in Gaza.

El-Sayed said a vote for his rival Rogers “is going to be a vote for Donald Trump” as his campaign reportedly saw a fundraising surge after Stevens crossed party lines.

Democratic leaders have rallied behind El-Sayed, who has faced Islamophobic attacks during the campaign.

The chairman of the Democratic Party in Michigan criticised Stevens. “She does not speak for that larger community,” Curtis Hertel said in an interview with CNN.

The leader of the Democrats in the Senate, Chuck Schumer, slammed Stevens over her endorsement of the Republican candidate.

“This is a despicable betrayal from Representative Stevens,” he posted on X.

“Senate Democrats are united in our support for Abdul El-Sayed, because we know he will fight for the economic interests of working families in Michigan, unlike Mike Rogers, who is a tool of Donald Trump and billionaire donors.”

Meanwhile, the advocacy group Michigan Jews for Democracy has backed El-Sayed.

“Haley Stevens does not speak for Michigan’s Jewish community,” it said in a statement, which rejected her “attempt to divide our community” two days before the election.

While Democrats have been angered by Stevens’s action, Trump celebrated her decision.

During a rally in Iowa later on Sunday, the US president said he would “love” it if Stevens joined the Republican Party. “Haley said what few people are willing to say,” Trump added. “It’s a thing called bravery.”

The question now is whether Stevens’s endorsement could persuade some of her supporters to back Rogers or discourage them from voting altogether.

Polling suggested El-Sayed has largely consolidated Democratic support, according to the Reuters news agency.

“I’m going to accept the election results,” Sullivan, an ally of Trump, told CNN on Sunday.

This year, out of about 210 million registered voters, 54.5 million had voted early as of 10am US East Coast time (15:00 GMT) on Sunday, according to a University of Florida analysis. This includes 30 million in-person votes and 24 million mail-in ballots.

In the 2022 midterm elections, 46 million people voted early.

Early voting enthusiasm has been higher among Democrats this year with more than 11 million registered Democrats having voted early, compared with about 8 million registered Republicans, according to the university’s analysis.

According to an Associated Press polling analysis, in South Carolina, where Trump won by nearly 18 percentage points in 2024, early voting has surged 83 percent compared with the same point in 2022. The state has open races for governor and US Senate this year.

#4. Nine killed as small jet crashes on Florida street

Dashcam footage captures Florida private jet crash

A business jet crashed in a neighbourhood in Florida on Sunday, killing all nine people on board, the US transport secretary has said.

The crash happened near Cresthaven, some nine miles (14 km) from Mar-a-Lago, President Donald Trump's resort in the wealthy enclave of Palm Beach County, around 12:50 EST (17:50 GMT).

Images show a large crater in the middle of a multi-lane road, with fire and debris from the Learjet 60 aircraft scattered across it, but nobody on the ground was hurt.

The aircraft had departed from Austin, Texas, and was headed to the Bahamas, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) said in a statement.

Flight analysis indicates that the jet was plummeting hundreds of feet per second before crashing.

In air traffic control audio recorded moments before the crash, someone in the cockpit can be heard reporting "moderate turbulence" and that the crew was "managing the ice".

The person can then be heard saying the plane was "picking up precipitation" and "we need to climb". Moments later, the same voice said the plane was descending at a rate of 10,000ft (3,048m) per minute.

The crew member declared an emergency, yelling "we are in an overspeed condition descending 20,000ft per minute", before saying, "we are going to crash into the ground".

About 35 seconds after the initial mayday announcement, the last remark from the cockpit was "we're out of time".

A flaming crater at the scene of the crash in Florida

Unverified footage posted on social media shows what appears to be a dash-camera recording of the moment of the crash. It shows vehicles driving on a residential suburban street, when suddenly an aircraft nosedives into the ground almost vertically, and explodes into a large fireball.

"When you see that video, it's a jarring thing," Florida Governor Ron DeSantis told reporters at a news conference after the crash. "It's like a missile coming down, and a massive explosion, debris going everywhere."

It is not yet clear who was aboard the jet when it crashed. DeSantis said "as of now, we don't have any reports of anyone who was on the ground...who was killed or injured".

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy described the crash as a "horrific tragedy", and said the FAA was sending a team to the scene to help an investigation by the National Transportation Safety Board.

"Please join me in praying for the families and loved ones of those we lost," Duffy added.

Palm Beach County Fire Rescue has declared a "mass casualty incident".

The Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office said the crash happened on Military Trail, a six-lane road that features businesses and restaurants.

At a news conference, DeSantis and local officials said that 600 local residents were being evacuated from a nearby trailer-park community.

The impact of the crash caused considerable damage to local infrastructure, including its water lines, they said, and it would be at least 30 days before the road re-opened.

Thunderstorms had been reported in the area at the time of the crash, and it was heavily raining as the news conference was held.

Trump had been at Mar-a-Lago on Sunday morning, and departed from an airport nearby on Air Force One about an hour before the crash.

#5. MLB ‘spot-fixing’ trial set to begin in US court

MLB pitchers charged with conspiracy to influence sporting contests by bribery go on trial in New York.

Former Cleveland Guardians pitchers Emmanuel Clase and Luis Ortiz go on trial on Monday on charges of accepting bribes after colluding with gamblers who made hundreds of thousands of dollars by betting correctly on individual pitches in Major League Baseball (MLB) in the United States.

Their careers and possibly their freedom are at stake in the jury trial expected to unfold over the next month in New York’s Brooklyn federal court. Several professional baseball players, including catchers and baseball executives, are among witnesses expected to testify.

Clase, the Guardians’ former closer, and Ortiz, a starter, have pleaded not guilty to conspiracy charges and are on non-disciplinary leave from the club without pay while their fate is determined.

According to an indictment, the pitchers accepted thousands of dollars in payoffs to help two gamblers from their native Dominican Republic win at least $460,000 on in-game prop bets on the speed and outcome of certain pitches.

Prosecutors allege that Clase began providing the betters with information about his pitches in 2023 and recruited Ortiz into the scheme in 2025. Ortiz’s lawyers unsuccessfully sought to have him tried separately from Clase.

Judge Kiyo A Matsumoto said at a conference last Friday that she hopes to complete jury selection quickly enough that lawyers can deliver relatively short opening statements in the afternoon.

Clase, 28, is a three-time All-Star and two-time American League Reliever of the Year who had completed his fourth season of a $20m, five-year contract when charges against the pitchers were revealed in November 2025.

Ortiz, 27, had earned $782,600 as a starting pitcher for the Guardians during the 2025 season.

The pitchers have been charged with wire fraud conspiracy, honest services wire fraud conspiracy, money laundering conspiracy and conspiracy to influence sporting contests by bribery.

Defence lawyers are expected to argue that the pitchers did not collude with gamblers and instead were varying the placement of pitches strategically to fool batters.

On Friday, the judge ruled that prosecutors cannot show the jury proof that Clase owed $500,000 in US taxes because they had only provided the evidence to defence lawyers several days earlier and long after a deadline had passed to turn over evidence to their adversaries.

A prosecutor had argued that the government wanted to put the evidence in the trial to offset any claim by Clase’s lawyers that he had no incentive to collude with gamblers because he had several million dollars in financial accounts in the Dominican Republic.

MLB and the Guardians have said they have fully cooperated with authorities. MLB said it contacted federal law enforcement when it began investigating unusual betting activity.

#6. Iran killed her teenage daughter - now she is facing up to the regime

Samira says she never used to be political.

"I worked in sales in Iran. I sold cosmetics," she tells BBC Panorama.

But that all changed the day her only child was shot dead in front of her by Iranian security forces. Sixteen-year-old Sevda was taking part in protests against Iran's repressive regime that engulfed the country earlier this year - before the US and Israel launched widespread air strikes which killed the country's supreme leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.

Samira's grief became fury as Iran's authorities then hindered, rather than helped, her search for her daughter's body, she says.

"I realised that finding Sevda wasn't the end, but just the beginning of all the harassment they put me through."

Samira made a dramatic escape from Iran on foot, navigating dangerous mountainous terrain.

She now lives abroad in hiding, describing herself as a "one-woman army fighting the regime". In a search for accountability and justice after her daughter's death, she is intent on naming the members of the security forces and officials she believes were responsible for firing fatal shots during the protests.

"They'll bring me to the brink of death before they kill me, but finding me won't be easyâ¦ Return to normal life? There is no going back," she says.

Samira is among several Iranians the BBC has spoken to since the start of the year. Some, still inside the country, told Panorama they wanted to speak out despite the risks, saying they had found it difficult to hold on to hope once the mass protests had been brutally crushed.

The Iranian embassy in London told Panorama that peaceful and legitimate protests had been hijacked by foreign powers, including the US, which promoted violence, provided weapons and equipment, and compromised public security.

Sevda was killed on 9 January as she and Samira took part in huge demonstrations on the outskirts of the capital Tehran which had begun the previous month. They had been sparked by economic discontent, before escalating into calls for an end to the country's Islamic Republic.

Though Samira denies she was especially political then, she says her daughter was. Sevda's idols were two other young women - Mahsa Amini, whose death in custody after an alleged violation against the mandatory headscarf triggered mass protests, and Nika Shakarami, who was killed during a subsequent uprising against Iran's strict Islamic dress code for women.

"She kept them alive in her thoughts and always wanted to continue their path [of resistance]," Samira says.

The day before Sevda was killed, the numbers protesting had been particularly high. Samira says she and Sevda had responded to a call by Reza Pahlavi, the exiled son of Iran's last shah (king), for demonstrations.

"The security forces had lost control," she says.

The swell in numbers had spooked Iran's government, and the following day a shoot-to-kill order was announced, Samira says. But she had thought it just an empty threat.

"They lied so much in Iranâ¦ We couldn't imagine a massacre of that scale."

And they were reassured, she says, by US President Trump's promise that he had their backs.

He had told US media on 8 January: "I have let [the Iranian authorities] know that if they start killing people, which they tend to do during their riotsâ¦ we're going to hit them very hard."

But nevertheless the crowds appeared smaller on the 9th, Samira says.

Sevda seemed to know the risks, she says. Her daughter took off her earrings and gave them to her grandmother. She also shared her phone passcode with Samira.

The mother and daughter gathered with the rest of the protesters. Sevda then stood on a dustbin and began leading a chant.

"It seemed there were more forces than people," Samira says of the area where they were. "I don't know how, within one day, they gathered so manyâ¦ Basijis and IRGC [Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps] forces."

The IRGC defends the country's Islamic establishment, and the paramilitary Basij Resistance Force are volunteers for the IRGC.

"A sniper fired from the roof of a building on our right. The bullet must have come from the rooftop. The bullet went through her heart. I couldn't even scream. I just stared at Sevda - her body lying lifeless. Blood was gushing out her mouth and nose. I couldn't believe it, I couldn't believe she was dying. Her eyes were still looking at me."

Samira says she managed to get Sevda to hospital and she was told they were operating on her, but then the door opened and she saw Sevda had been zipped into a body bag.

Sevda's body was taken away. Samira says it took her days to find her daughter again. Like many others, she found herself at one of Tehran's largest morgues.

"Teenagers - young boys and girls. So many had been killed," she cries.

"I died with every bag I unzipped. I'd never seen so many bodies. But I wouldn't give up."

She finally found her daughter. "Three bodies were piled on top of Sevda. That's how we found her."

Iranian authorities acknowledge that more than 3,000 people were killed in the protests, but claim some were members of the security forces. One human rights group, Hrana, however, estimates the number of dead stands at more than 7,000, most of them protesters.

Finding Sevda's body was just the beginning of Samira's battle with the authorities, she says.

To be allowed to take away the body for burial, she says she was forced to say that "terrorists" rather than the security forces had been responsible for Sevda's death.

"They did this to cover up what they'd done on 8 and 9 January, to hide how many people they had killed. They silenced families by telling them to say their loved ones were killed by Israeli or American terrorists."

Nevertheless she was barred from visiting the grave after the funeral, or organising a tombstone, she says.

And Samira says the harassment continued. A few days before the ceremony to mark 40 days since Sevda died - highly significant in Iranian culture - she says she was summoned by a local branch of the Ministry of Intelligence.

"They told me: 'You should give us an interview and say that Sevda was a Basiji and she was killed by the protesters. Then we will announce her [as] a martyr - you will then receive perks, a house, or anything that you want.'"

She is sure this offer was prompted by fear, because she had shared Sevda's death with an Iranian media organisation based in London, once the internet had been reconnected in the country.

"It scared them that I was in touch with external networks or those outside Iran."

To avoid giving the interview, Samira fled Iran as soon as the 40th day anniversary was over.

"I didn't want to trade with my child's blood."

She says people smugglers helped her to escape. She crossed rivers and mountains to get to safety.

A video she made of herself fleeing makes clear her fury.

"This crap government, these bastards, they've harassed me so much," she tells the camera as she marches along a mountain path bordered by a deep ravine.

To mark what would have been Sevda's 17th birthday, her relatives and friends held a vigil - with Samira watching from exile on a livestream.

#8. Democrats have high hopes for US midterms - but are the polls right?

The Democratic Party has high expectations as it heads into these pivotal US midterm elections.

A historically unpopular Republican president, an American public unhappy with the rising costs of food and bills, and months of favourable polls point to a good night for them on Tuesday.

Democratic candidates lead in well over the 218 seats needed to flip control of the lower chamber of Congress, the House of Representatives, the polls suggest. And they are tied or narrowly ahead in the key Senate races.

A recent New York Times poll had the Senate race in South Carolina, a state Donald Trump won by 18 percentage points in 2024, as a tie. Recent CNN surveys put the Democrats ahead in Texas, Kansas and Maine.

Recent US political history is littered with examples where public opinion polls, even ones just days before an election, were off the mark. Trump outperformed predictions on his way to White House victories in 2016 and 2024. His narrow defeat in 2020 came after most surveys indicated a more comfortable win for Democrat Joe Biden.

Earlier this year, polls suggested Democrat Abdul El-Sayed was poised for a double-digit win in Michigan's Democratic Senate primary. Instead it was 1%. In Wisconsin's Democratic primary for governor, the candidate that had a polling lead that approached 20 points lost by a fraction of a percent.

Democrats have an identity crisis - it won't be an easy fix

According to Clifford Young, president of Ipsos Public Affairs, which conducts political and market research surveys, polling has become difficult in recent years because it's harder to reach Americans as they abandon their land lines and screen their calls. And many of those who pollsters do contact have become less willing to participate.

"Conservatives, especially those on the far right, are much more anti-establishment by nature," Young says. "And they don't participate in anything. They hate the system. That's the challenge."

If Republican voters are being undercounted in a systematic way, and polling companies don't take that into consideration when constructing their voting models, it could skew their data towards the Democrats.

That's one of the reasons why it has become trickier for pollsters to predict what the electorate will look like.

#9. Anthrax outbreak kills hippos in DR Congo’s Virunga National Park

Drought and conflict compound the threat to wildlife in eastern DR Congo.

Vitshumbi, DR Congo – While the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) continues to grapple with the deadliest Ebola outbreak in its history, another disease is taking a heavy toll on wildlife in Virunga National Park in the country’s east.

Over the past four weeks, hippopotamus carcasses have been discovered in the Semuliki River, west of Africa’s oldest national park, raising concern among communities living along its banks.

“It was in mid-September that we began to notice the mass deaths of hippos in the Semuliki River. We have started to fear this situation, which is unfolding whilst we are in the midst of the Ebola outbreak,” Michael Kambale, a resident of the Vitshumbi enclave, about 100 kilometres (62 miles) north of Goma, the capital of North Kivu province, told Al Jazeera.

Following laboratory tests, Virunga National Park confirmed that anthrax, caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis, was responsible for the wildlife deaths.

According to park officials, 28 hippos died over approximately one month.

“This disease can be transmitted to humans through contact with a dead or sick animal or by consuming its meat,” Virunga National Park said in a press release sent to Al Jazeera.

Joel Mbusa Mapoli, an expert in ecology and veterinary medicine, said the deaths represented a significant loss for Virunga National Park and called for urgent action to contain the outbreak.

He explained that hippos act as ecosystem engineers, altering nutrient flows and the ecological conditions of their habitats.

Virunga National Park said it was focusing on surveillance, the safe management of carcasses and reducing risks to wildlife and local communities.

“Teams are monitoring the affected area, recording new cases of mortality and coordinating the safe handling and disposal of carcasses,” the park said.

“People living in the affected area and its surroundings are being informed about the outbreak; they are advised not to touch, skin or consume animals found dead, and to report any carcasses or suspected cases to the relevant authorities,” the park added.

Founded in 1925, Virunga is Africa’s oldest national park and is renowned for its biodiversity.

Olivier Ndoole Bahemuke, a Congolese environmental activist and head of Alerte Congolaise pour l’environnement et les droits de l’homme, a nonprofit organisation operating in the DRC, said the armed conflict that has persisted in the country for nearly three decades had fuelled poaching and deforestation.

The park says it has lost control of a significant part of its territory over the past four years because of the ongoing conflict between the Congolese army and M23 fighters.

The M23 armed group says it is helping protect the area from poachers and other potential human threats.

Park officials said anthrax outbreaks had previously been observed among hippos and other wild animals in the ecosystem during periods of drought.

According to  Mapoli, several factors contribute to recurring anthrax outbreaks in Virunga National Park. These include the persistence of the bacterium in the environment, deteriorating ecological and climatic conditions, the concentration of animals around aquatic habitats and difficulties in monitoring wildlife health.

“When vegetation becomes scarce, herbivores may graze closer to the ground and congregate around the few remaining grazing areas and water points, thereby increasing their exposure to contaminated soil,” Virunga National Park said in a statement sent to Al Jazeera, adding that the situation was being monitored “closely”.

The park’s February 2026 census estimated the hippo population at more than 2,700, distributed across 171 groups.

In 2025, an anthrax outbreak killed 65 hippos in Virunga National Park, according to the park’s February 2026 population update. Park staff mobilised excavators to retrieve the carcasses from the water.

According to park officials, anthrax can survive for decades in the soil in the form of spores.

Mapoli said the response to anthrax should combine conservation measures with public health interventions, in line with the One Health principle, an approach that recognises the links between human, animal and environmental health.

He called for stronger epidemiological surveillance, better protection for local communities, proper management of animal carcasses and greater collaboration among state, non-state and para-state bodies.

“The hippopotamus is one of the flagship species of the Virunga ecosystems. Its disappearance could lead to a major disruption of aquatic ecosystems, as these animals play a role in the exchange of matter between terrestrial and aquatic environments,” he told Al Jazeera.

#19. How the president’s party has fared in every US midterm election since 1946

Only twice in the past 20 midterms has the president’s party gained House seats. Polls suggest 2026 won’t be the third.

On Tuesday, Americans head to the polls for the United States midterm elections, held halfway through a president’s four-year term. Voters will choose all 435 members of the US House of Representatives and 35 of the Senate’s 100 seats. The voting will decide which party controls Congress for the next two years.

Although the president’s name won’t appear on the ballot, the vote is widely seen as a verdict on the presidency, and the president’s party almost always pays the price.

In 18 of the 20 midterms held from 1946 to 2022, voters took House seats away from the party in control of the White House. The average loss was 26 seats. Only Bill Clinton’s Democrats in 1998 and George W Bush’s Republicans in 2002 bucked the trend.

Political scientists call this the “presidential penalty”, or the “midterm penalty”. The president’s party tends to lose seats simply because it holds power as voters use the midterms to put a check on the White House.

The chart below shows how many House seats the president’s party has won or lost in each midterm since 1946 from Harry S Truman to Joe Biden.

Democratic presidents have suffered the biggest midterm defeats since 1946. On average, their party has lost about 29 House seats in a midterm, compared with about 23 under Republican presidents.

The largest loss came in 2010, two years after Barack Obama became the first African American elected president, when Democrats lost 64 House seats and Republicans took control of the chamber. The Democrats under Truman lost 56 seats in 1946, and under Clinton, they lost 54 in 1994, each time losing control of the House.

The worst Republican defeats came under Dwight D Eisenhower when the party lost 50 seats in 1958 and Gerald Ford when it lost 48 in 1974. Donald Trump’s first midterm in 2018 cost Republicans 42 seats and control of the House.

Big losses often cost the president’s party its majority. In seven of the 20 midterms since 1946 (1946, 1954, 1994, 2006, 2010, 2018 and 2022), the House changed hands.

The president’s party has lost Senate seats in 13 of the 20 midterms since 1946 but lost control of the chamber in only six: 1946, 1954, 1986, 1994, 2006 and 2014.

Unlike the House of Representatives, where every seat is up for election every two years, only about a third of the Senate’s 100 seats are contested in each cycle because senators serve six-year terms and their elections are staggered. That means which states are voting can matter as much as the national mood.

Eisenhower’s Republicans suffered the biggest Senate loss in the period, giving up 13 seats in 1958. Truman’s Democrats lost 12 in 1946, and Obama’s Democrats lost nine in 2014, handing control of the chamber to Republicans.

At times, the two chambers have moved in opposite directions. In 1982, Ronald Reagan’s Republicans lost 26 House seats but gained one in the Senate. In 2018, Trump’s Republicans lost the House but added a Senate seat, and in 2022, Biden’s Democrats lost the House while gaining one seat in the Senate.

These split results show that a backlash against the president does not land evenly across Congress. A party can hold its ground on a favourable Senate map while losing competitive House districts.

Losing seats and losing control are not the same thing. Lyndon Johnson’s Democrats lost 47 House seats in 1966 but kept their majority. Twenty years later, Reagan’s Republicans lost only four House seats but lost the Senate after an eight-seat setback.

Control of the chambers decides who sets the agenda and passes legislation. A bill must pass both chambers before it reaches the president for his approval or veto, so an opposition majority in either can block the president’s plans.

A House majority can also mean a party is able to launch investigations and impeach a president while a Senate majority can reject the president’s nominees, including federal judges, who serve for life.

This year, Democrats need to gain three seats to win the House and four to win the Senate. A 50-50 split in the Senate would leave Republicans in control because Vice President JD Vance may cast tie-breaking votes.

#21. RFK Jr is wrong about the Arab world

The challenges in knowledge production in Arab societies cannot be blamed on some kind of innate incompetence or a single political group.

At a recent campaign rally in Michigan, United States Secretary of Health Robert F Kennedy Jr made remarks that have caused much controversy. He alleged that Muslim Arabs do not produce science and literature and do not win major awards because of the influence of the Muslim Brotherhood.

This was not merely a hasty and inaccurate assessment of the knowledge production of a specific demographic. It was a statement that reflects racist attitudes and misconceptions about an entire region and an entire religion. It is also easily refutable.

There are plenty of Muslim Arab scientists, thinkers and writers who have made major contributions over the past century. Some of them are Nobel laureates, such as novelist Naguib Mahfouz, diplomat Mohamed ElBaradei, journalist Tawakkol Karman, and scientists Ahmed Zewail, Moungi Bawendi and Omar Yaghi. Many others have not received a Western award but have nevertheless contributed significantly to their own countries and the world.

The Arab world does face challenges in knowledge production, but this does not prove an inherent deficit in its people or the overwhelming influence of a certain political group. Rather, it reflects historical and institutional conditions that impact this process.

Human civilisations have not been equal in knowledge production across history. Some have witnessed scientific and intellectual flourishing while others have faced decline and turmoil.

Downturns have not occurred because populations had suddenly lost their mental capacities but because the political, economic, cultural and institutional conditions that allow knowledge to thrive change over time.

In the case of the Islamic civilisation, its centres – from Baghdad and Damascus to Cairo and Cordoba – experienced a vast scientific and intellectual movement involving scholars, translators, philosophers, physicians and mathematicians from diverse religious and linguistic backgrounds for centuries. This prosperity was not isolated from the world; it was built on the translation, critique, development and interaction with Greek, Syriac, Indian and other bodies of knowledge.

Meanwhile, parts of Europe experienced significant backwardness in scientific education and development. However, Europe was not a monolith, nor were those centuries a homogeneous dark period. It also witnessed varying intellectual, institutional and scientific developments across different regions and timeframes.

European conditions then gradually changed: Educational institutions and universities evolved, networks for book and knowledge circulation expanded, and European scientific traditions interacted with scientific knowledge transferred from the Islamic world and elsewhere.

No one can claim, based on these historical disparities, that Europeans are inherently incapable of producing science or that their religion or ethnic origins preclude them from innovation. Serious historical interpretation focuses on institutions, economics, education, political patronage and knowledge networks rather than claims that certain populations lack innate abilities.

The exact same principle must apply to Arabs and Muslims today. Since Europe was able to change the conditions of knowledge production, it is illogical to assume that the contemporary reality of Arab and Islamic societies reflects fixed limits in their scientific or literary capacities.

Science is universal and not the racial or religious property of anyone. Decline is not a genetic destiny for any people, and prosperity is not an eternal privilege.

Rejecting RFK Jr’s generalisations does not mean denying the challenges facing scientific research in many Arab countries. There are serious questions concerning research and development funding, university independence, education quality, freedom of research and expression, institutional stability, the ability to attract and retain scientists, and links between knowledge and the economy and production. However, these questions are the beginning of scientific analysis, not the end.

A society that does not provide a researcher with a suitable laboratory, does not grant universities sufficient independence, does not allocate the necessary resources for research and does not ensure the continuity of scientific institutions cannot expect results comparable to those of a society that invests sustainably in these areas.

Furthermore, wars, conflicts, displacement, brain drain and weak scientific cooperation can disrupt the accumulation of knowledge, even when a society possesses qualified human capital. The capacity for thought and innovation is one thing, and the ability to translate them into sustainable scientific production is another. Between the two lies an entire system of socioeconomic conditions, institutions and policies.

Blaming the challenges contemporary Arab societies face on a single political group – in RFK Jr’s case, the Muslim Brotherhood – and presenting this as a comprehensive explanation for the state of science and literature among hundreds of millions of people falls short of serious historical or social analysis.

Scientific production is influenced by a set of intersecting variables: state structure, economic policies, education quality, research expenditures, academic freedom, stability, international relations and the capacity for knowledge circulation.

Certain political movements or intellectual trends might impact educational policies or the cultural climate in specific contexts. However, proving this impact requires evidence and comparative analysis, which RFK Jr would likely fail to produce, should he be asked to do so.

Moving from critiquing an institution or political movement to issuing a judgement on Arabs or Muslims in general is a form of racism, hatred and Islamophobia because it transitions from analysis to generalisation, and from seeking causes to reproducing stereotypes.

The problem with RFK Jr’s statement is not merely its factual inaccuracy regarding awards and their superficiality, but the logic upon which it is based: transforming historical disparities in knowledge production into broad judgements about peoples.

His words hold symbolic, intellectual and political significance, reflecting a hostile Orientalist stance towards Arab-Islamic and even Eastern heritage.

A serious response to these statements requires more than merely recalling past glories or listing Arab and Muslim scientists and thinkers; it requires turning the discussion into a project for the future.

In the Arab world, we need more independent universities capable of research, equipped laboratories, sustainable funding, an environment that permits critique and experimentation, policies that retain scientific talent and institutions that link education with the economy and innovation.

We also need a documented, critical rereading of the history of sciences in Islamic civilisation – one that studies both achievements and failures and examines how scientific concepts emerged, evolved and were transferred between civilisations.

A civilisation does not regain its stature by repeating that its ancestors were great but by building the institutions that allow new generations to produce new knowledge.

#27. The future of the Palestinian vote in Israel

The backlash against the high Palestinian turnout reveals much about the state of Israeli democracy and its future.

In the October 27 Israeli elections, 38 parties ran for office. Of these, only two supported peace, equality and an end to the occupation of Palestinian territories. Both were “Arab parties” – made up of Palestinian citizens of Israel.

These two parties earned a historic result, securing 19 seats in the Knesset, after some 80 percent of Palestinian citizens cast a vote.

This unprecedented turnout contributed to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s failure to secure a majority.  His bloc won 53 seats, while the Jewish-led opposition won 48.

The electoral mobilisation of Palestinians was immediately spun as a threat and Palestinian communities now face intensified incitement by members of Netanyahu’s camp. The pro-Netanyahu camp has alleged that the election was “stolen” and is now calling for restrictions on the “Arab vote”.

Thus, Palestinian electoral success exposed an uncomfortable question for Israeli politics: what happens when citizens who are informally isolated on the political scene but whose vote is formally equal become powerful enough to determine who governs?

Palestinian citizens face a fundamental question: Can they truly determine the political outcome and if they can, does that make them equal citizens?

Palestinian citizens of Israel have long faced a dilemma over voting. If they do not mobilise to vote, the risk is that the Jewish right would come to power is high. If they do vote, there is no guarantee that their participation would translate into policies that protect or benefit their communities.

In 1999, Arab turnout reached approximately 75 percent in the elections and Palestinian citizens overwhelmingly supported Ehud Barak of the Labour Party against Netanyahu. A year later, after Ariel Sharon’s visit to Al-Aqsa compound and the outbreak of the second Intifada, Israeli police killed 13 Palestinian citizens. Outrage and disillusionment contributed to an Arab boycott of the 2001 election, when turnout fell to about 19 percent. Sharon won.

Today, Palestinian electoral participation still retains its sway. Higher turnout among Palestinians means fewer seats for two main blocs – the pro- and anti-Netanyahu camps – which could tip the balance in the Knesset. This is what happened on October 27 .

But this potentially powerful position of the Palestinian parties has been diminished by the refusal of Jewish-led parties to work with them on government formation.

The logic of this exclusion was made clear in April 2021, when far-right politician Bezalel Smotrich rejected Netanyahu’s attempt to establish a government supported by one of the Palestinian parties, Ra’am. Smotrich argued that doing so would encourage greater Palestinian turnout which would allow the Palestinian community to enable the return of the left to power in Israel. Accepting Palestinian parties would therefore challenge the Jewish right’s political dominance.

Ra’am eventually joined the short-lived coalition government formed by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, without making any national demands, but its participation came at the cost of significant concessions on national issues. For the first time, Palestinian political leaders recognised Israel as a Jewish state and were even willing to set aside the national question in favour of addressing civil and socioeconomic issues.

Despite these concessions, the Bennett–Lapid government ultimately collapsed following the resignation of two coalition members who opposed sitting in a government alongside Palestinian representatives.

Netanyahu then realised that to prevent the formation of any future government of change, he needed to delegitimise the Palestinian parties and limit their political representation.

Following October 7, 2023, Palestinian citizens experienced a sharp contraction of political space. Students faced disciplinary proceedings over social-media posts; employees lost jobs; antiwar speech prompted police investigations and arrests.

The electoral process itself reflected this climate. Israel’s Central Elections Committee voted to disqualify both Palestinian electoral lists, along with lawmaker Ofer Cassif and Balad chairman Sami Abu Shehadeh. The Supreme Court eventually reversed the bans on the lists.

Meanwhile, attacking Palestinians, including Palestinian citizens of Israel, became a political staple. Ahead of this election, media outlet Arabic Post conducted an analysis of more than 10,000 election-related social-media items. It reported that 40 percent of posts by candidates from Netanyahu’s camp concerned Arabs or Palestinians, and that 94 percent of those were hostile.

More troubling were the police raids on the homes of Ra’am activists in the Negev, following suspicions of electoral bribery which led to the arrest of three men.

Police acknowledged searches without warrants and later reports questioned the strength of the evidence. All three men were released. But the ripple effect of those allegations helped shape a narrative of suspicion against the Palestinian community.

In this election, after years of repression and incitement, Palestinian citizens did not retreat. They organised, voted in unprecedented numbers and achieved a remarkable result. They took a gamble by taking their citizenship seriously and by asserting their right to full political equality and a meaningful say in shaping the country’s future.

However, the parties they voted for face isolation unless they fulfil the conditions set by the Jewish-led opposition: namely, recognising Israel as exclusively Jewish, labelling Hamas a “terrorist” organisation and accepting compulsory military or national service, from which Palestinian citizens are now exempt.

In other words, Palestinians can access the political power they should have only if they fully capitulate on the Palestinian question, embrace Zionism and accept their own status as unequal citizens.

In parallel, the Palestinian community also faces intensified incitement, particularly by the pro-Netanyahu camp. Politicians and public figures are throwing claims of electoral manipulation, insisting “the Arabs stole the election”.

Former Deputy Knesset Speaker Moshe Feiglin went as far as declaring that the Palestinian electoral mobilisation was the “October 7” of Israeli politics. Yair Netanyahu, the prime minister’s son, called Palestinian citizens of Israel “the greatest danger to the survival of the State of Israel—aside from the Iranian nuclear threat”.

Many Palestinian citizens now fear that political success could bring further intimidation, restrictions or violence rather than recognition.

Thus, the high voter turnout among Palestinian citizens has forced Israeli society and its political establishment to confront the inherent contradiction between Israel’s identity as a Jewish state and its claim to be a democracy.

This fundamental dilemma may ultimately be resolved in favour of Jewish political dominance, leading to legislation that further undermines Palestinian civil rights and expands the system of apartheid and Jewish supremacy.

It would not be surprising if a bill were introduced in the next Knesset aimed solely at restricting Palestinians’ voting rights. There are already calls on social media for such a move.

A July survey by Israel Democracy Institute found that only 46 percent of respondents in the right-wing Jewish camp supported Palestinian citizens’ right to vote.

In the current reality, following the genocide in Gaza, there is a growing sense among Jewish Israelis that there are no longer any limits to what they can do, pushing the state to act with virtually no restraint towards Palestinian citizens.

If Israeli society does not change direction, its pretence of democracy may soon no longer hold.

#33. Shadowy Indian election ‘consultants’ add US midterms to global footprint

India’s political consultancies grow by covertly managing the world’s biggest elections. Now, they are exporting the dirty tricks to manipulate global elections, like the US midterms.

New Delhi, India — In an unfurnished flat in a nondescript Ahmedabad neighbourhood in western India, a 22-year-old digital content creator worked through the nights, designing posters and editing videos for a presidential campaign thousands of kilometres away — William Ruto’s 2022 bid for the presidency in Kenya.

Rajan Kumar’s content was not shared by Ruto’s official campaign. Instead, the 50 social media posts he created for $7 a day were shared on shadow social media accounts that conceal their identity to make coordinated messaging appear independent while backing Ruto’s campaign.

For content, Kumar said he would trim opposing leaders’ speeches or interviews to twist their statements, or create posters with false claims amplifying Ruto’s prospects and popularity ahead of the election.

“If there’s a woman politician, we simply link her to someone to damage her reputation. It’s one of the easiest ways to discredit,” Kumar told Al Jazeera, adding that “all controversial content” is diverted to shadow pages for “extensive distribution”.

Ruto won the 2022 election and became Kenya’s fifth president.

Four years later, as the United States heads into its midterm elections on Tuesday, researchers have found that Indian social media bots and accounts have been shadow-posting videos backing specific campaigns. The New York Times first reported such posts supporting US Democratic politician Haley Stevens ahead of the August 4 Senate primary in Michigan, which she lost to Abdul El-Sayed, the party’s candidate for the midterm election.

But the US is only the latest theatre of electoral war where politicians are banking on Indian social media mercenaries-for-hire, who have fought shadow election campaigns in several countries in South America and Africa.

Nearly a dozen political consultants in India, who spoke with Al Jazeera for this report, say there are no limits, guardrails, or red lines in such shadow campaigns.

“We are manipulating elections; there is no other way to say it,” said Tushar Giri, a political consultant in New Delhi. “The concept of electoral democracy is so overrated. There is no free will for voters. Everything is manipulated.”

Political consultancies advise politicians and election campaigns on how to win public support and elections. Such specialised private companies selling political persuasion as a service emerged in the US in the 1930s.

With the boom in social media, dirty tricks have spread to online platforms to manipulate and attract voters, especially by running targeted advertising campaigns on Meta and Google platforms to reach millions of voters. Previous reports have found that Russia used similar tactics to influence voters in the US, for instance.

Strategies for dissemination of content deemed too controversial for official accounts during election campaigns vary, ranging from political consultancies taking over a party’s established shadow pages on Meta or Google to making new ones from scratch, rebranding existing pages to suit a campaign’s needs, or opting for pages for hire.

An Al Jazeera investigation in the run-up to India’s 2024 national election revealed a sprawling network of such shadowy pages on Meta and Google products like Facebook, Instagram and YouTube on sale — skirting the platforms’ policies against such misuse of targeted advertisements to influence voters.

Antibot4Navalny, an anonymous group of disinformation researchers that investigates and exposes coordinated online influence operations, particularly those linked to Russia, told Al Jazeera that it found at least 28 such accounts on X (formerly Twitter) sharing videos supporting Stevens’s campaign in the Democratic primary in Michigan, garnering more than seven million views.

At least 15 of the identified accounts were posting from India, a review by Al Jazeera has found.

Researchers at Clemson University’s Media Forensic Hub have also identified similar tactics being used by for-hire Indian accounts and pages to support Stevens’s campaign against El-Sayed.

Amogh Dhar Sharma, author of Backstage of Democracy — a book on Indian political consultancies’ workings, said politicians like to keep voters’ focus ahead of elections “on things that happen in the public eye”.

“But sometimes the real power is being wielded, completely hidden from the public eye,” Sharma told Al Jazeera, referring to political consultancies. “The way it creates certain, hidden sources of power in a democracy is one of the biggest ethical concerns.”

In India, such political consultancies now make up the backbone of any election campaign, and their work serves as marketing material to lure politicians in other countries.

“We have a proven track record of delivering desired results in a complicated electoral democracy like India, which now attracts foreign campaigns too,” said Giri, the New Delhi-based consultant.

Rules set by Meta, Facebook’s parent firm, disallow users from any “attempt to or successfully sell, buy, or exchange” accounts, or from operating under false or stolen identities. In general, both Meta and Google’s policies require political advertisers to verify their identities and disclose who paid for election advertisements, though specific rules vary from country to country.

But Indian consultants tell Al Jazeera that these community standards are routinely violated.

“These platforms have long pages of such rules, but when you read between the lines, there are several loopholes one can exploit,” said Giri. Premium virtual private networks (VPNs) are the go-to tools for such consultancies when they face restrictions in one geography. Sometimes, changing an account’s country of origin simply does the job, enabling a person sitting in a foreign country to run targeted political advertisements.

Giri recalled meeting representatives of a presidential campaign in Suriname last year, although negotiations ultimately fell through. Sharma, the author, told Al Jazeera that he found Indian consultancies working in Australia and Germany as well during his research. Consultants told Al Jazeera that they have similarly worked on campaigns in India’s neighbouring countries, including Nepal and Sri Lanka.

Kumar, who worked for Ruto’s campaign for the Kenyan presidency in 2022, said he was paid about $7 a day under a monthly contract and was asked to deliver up to 100 creatives on some days, coordinated via groups on Telegram.

He said he never worked directly with any of Ruto’s representatives, and that content creators like him are never informed of the identities of all the individuals involved in the supply chain that leads up to senior officials in a campaign.

“One always needs to be careful and hide footprints nonetheless,” he said. Chats are deleted, and SIM cards are destroyed frequently.

Two Indian political consultants, Zulfiqar Ahmad Khan and Mohamed Zaid Sami Kidwai, who were working for Ruto’s campaign, went missing in Nairobi in July 2022 ahead of the election. They were found dead in October, allegedly abducted by an elite police unit in Kenya. After winning, Ruto disbanded that unit. Fifteen officers were accused of murder, but in August 2026, Kenya’s High Court terminated those charges after the prosecution requested that the murder investigation be discontinued.

The shadowy nature of the work that consultants like Kumar do extends to payments, several political consultants explained.

“Black money is a pillar of political consultation,” one consultant told Al Jazeera, requesting anonymity, fearing for their safety. “We have companies abroad that hire a person on paper for random work to make payments, but it is just a front to funnel money.”

Several consultancies rely on hawala networks — an informal, trust-based money-transfer system that allows people to send funds across borders through brokers, bypassing conventional banking channels.

At least three consultants identified export-focused businesses in Old Delhi’s sprawling Chandni Chowk market in the national capital territory as one such hub. A consultant who spoke with Al Jazeera for this report on condition of anonymity recalled a dramatic business deal two years ago.

The consultant carried an Indian banknote bearing a specific serial number, which served as a code to identify and collect the payment. “I saw people use 10-rupee notes to collect millions in cash,” they told Al Jazeera. “It was like I’m in a movie.” They refused to disclose the country or campaign that paid for this particular transaction.

Giri, the consultant, told Al Jazeera that in the last couple of years, India has turned into “a big wholesale market to run these shadow political campaigns”.

“It is like you buy toys from China; similarly, you can manipulate elections anywhere in the world at the cheapest cost in India,” he said.

Author Sharma had a similar analogy to explain the nature of India’s political consultancies.

“This is perhaps an extension of the global outsourcing industry that has been central to India’s economic growth,” Sharma argued. “Just as India became a hub for business-process outsourcing in the 2000s”, the country is now seeing the rise of political consultancies that campaigns can hire to outsource their less transparent operations.

Sharma added that the physical distance of those behind these shadowy social media promotions and the campaigns they work for gives everyone involved “plausible deniability” if operations are revealed.

Giri, the consultant, argued that what makes consultants successful is how far they are willing to go to help a campaign.

“What are the limits? If one agency can execute filthy tricks to manipulate a voter — and think beyond the political party’s own imagination — they are rewarded accordingly,” he said. “The dirtier you can get, the more money and opportunities there are.”

#36. A simple guide to the midterms

The US midterm elections take place on Tuesday, when voters take part in a ballot that could have a major impact on the rest of Donald Trump's presidency.

Americans will choose members of Congress, which is made up of two parts - the House of Representatives and the Senate.

Elections take place every two years, and when they happen in the middle of the president's four-year term of office, they are called the midterms.

A key task of Congress is to create and pass federal laws - laws that apply nationwide.

The outcome of the midterms will decide which political party - Trump's Republicans or opposition Democrats - controls Congress and how easy it is for the president to carry out his plans.

The results are also seen as a measure of how happy people in the US are with the president's performance so far.

All 435 members of the House - the lower chamber of Congress - will be elected. They serve fixed terms of two years. The House is commonly where proposed laws are introduced and debated.

Meanwhile, 35 of 100 seats in the Senate, the upper chamber, will also be up for grabs. Senators serve six-year terms. Among other jobs, they approve or block proposed laws that have been received from the House.

On the same day, voters across much of the US will also choose state-level politicians. Governor contests in California, Florida, Georgia and Michigan will be among the most closely watched.

The US economy is on the minds of many voters struggling with the price of fuel, groceries, housing and healthcare.

Inflation remains above the level targeted by the US Federal Reserve, partly down to the war that the US and Israel started with Iran in February.

Democrats argue Trump's programme of trade tariffs has also pushed up prices for Americans.

Immigration is another key issue. Republicans say Trump has delivered on a major campaign pledge in 2024 by tightening the border and launching a widespread - and often controversial - crackdown on illegal immigration.

Concerns over AI have grown in urgency in recent weeks. Senior politicians from both parties have agreed on the need to regulate it - setting themselves in opposition to Trump, who has rejected the idea of laws to control development of the technology.

Congress is one of the three branches of government, alongside the presidency and the judiciary. Control of it matters for the shaping of legislation.

Both the House and Senate are currently held by the Republican Party. But its majority is a slender one. They have 218 seats in the House, which is the minimum number needed to control it, plus one independent who votes with them.

In the Senate, they have 53 seats. The upper chamber will only change hands if the Democrats win 51 seats, which would overcome a tie-breaking vote by the vice-president - currently a Republican - in the event of both sides getting 50.

If opposition Democrats win back control of at least one chamber, they will gain power to bring their own laws to the floor.

Voters have tended to "punish" the party of the president in the past and vote against them.

A write-in candidate is someone whose name does not appear on the ballot but can still be chosen by voters who write it in themselves. #42. US midterms: What to know about independent voters and candidates

Despite a record number of independents, third-party candidates and voters struggle to penetrate the two-party political system.

As Americans head to the polls on Tuesday in the much-anticipated midterms, the record number of voters identifying as independent could prove decisive for control of the US Congress and state legislatures.

Nearly half of US voters – as many as 117 million adults – now identify as politically independent, with the two major parties, Democrats and Republicans, jockeying for their votes.

At the same time, independent or third-party candidates win few federal or state seats. The Democratic and Republican parties have dominated US politics, from state legislatures to the US Congress and the presidency, for generations.

“The whole [electoral] system is built to frustrate the independence of voters,” says Rick Bennett, the former Republican Party chair in Maine, where he is running to become only the third independent governor in the state’s history.

Bennett’s candidacy runs headlong into one of the most stubborn contradictions in US politics: While millions of eligible voters reject Republican and Democratic labels, far fewer will cast their ballots for independent candidates.

“For decades, candidates outside the Democratic and Republican parties have struggled to achieve [a high] level of support,” political scientist AJ Simmons said. “[But] 2026 is shaping up to be one of the strongest election cycles for independent candidates in recent memory.”

According to the Pew Research Center’s annual National Public Opinion Reference Survey, 44 percent of US adults identify as politically independent. This is in contrast to 29 percent who identify as Democrat and 28 percent as Republican.

But there is a caveat: Most self-described independents still gravitate towards one of the major parties. Independents who express absolutely no partisan leaning make up only about 7-10 percent of the total electorate.

These “true” independents are sought after by candidates because they are persuadable, the core of the coveted so-called swing vote in a deeply divided and partisan electorate and political system.

In the 2026 midterm elections, 52 percent of likely independent voters are supporting Democrats, while 39 percent back Republicans, polling firm Marist suggests.

Frustration with President Donald Trump is permeating the midterm electoral landscape, with independent respondents more than three times as likely to say their vote is mainly against the president, and 28 percent saying they are voting for “change”.

The US does not legally restrict elections to two parties, and many local officials are not party-affiliated.

But for more powerful federal and statewide seats like Tuesday’s House of Representatives and Senate races, the winner-take-all electoral system and single-member congressional districts work against outsiders and can discourage voters from backing them.

Even substantial support for a minor party is rarely concentrated enough in one district to win a congressional seat.

Voters, too, can unknowingly reinforce the system.

Polling trends and data further suggest that voters who identify as or prefer an independent will often instead back a Republican or Democrat they see as more politically viable. Voters are also reluctant to support “spoilers”, who may peel away votes from one of the two major-party candidates.

Bernard Tamas, a political scientist at Valdosta State University who studies third parties, said that although laws are often blamed for keeping third-party candidates off the ballot, “there is very little evidence that legal barriers are undermining these candidates”.

A total of $14.8bn was spent on federal elections during the 2024 presidential cycle, and the 2026 midterms are projected to surpass that, reaching up to $11.6bn in political advertisement spending alone.

The Democratic National Committee (DNC), which heads the Democratic Party, raised nearly $252.3m during the 2026 midterm campaign cycle while the Republican National Committee (RNC) took in about $341m.

Unlike other democracies, the US “offers virtually no public support” or funding to political parties and does not set limits on campaign spending”, Tamas said. Independent candidates “raise very little money” and are less well known by voters.

Lesley Shedd, a Republican strategist, told Al Jazeera that the surge in voters identifying as independents obscures their support for one of the two major parties.

“When somebody says they are independent, they really are a Republican or Democrat but they maybe don’t agree with the party on absolutely every issue,” Shedd said. “Or they [just] don’t want to feel as though they are part of the culture of the swamp of [Washington] DC.”

The real test for independent candidates is persuading Americans to vote for them, not just a “protest vote” against the system. And voters are reluctant to cast a “wasted” ballot, Simmons said, given the most competitive candidates are Democrats or Republicans.

There are efforts to support independent candidates. The Independent Leadership Council, launched in July by former Democrat-turned-independent Senator Joe Manchin, provides donors and campaign resources for independent congressional candidates. The project has raised more than $10m.

But the longer-term test, analysts maintain, is whether independent candidates can be viable across multiple races and election cycles.

There is currently one independent in the US House and two in the US Senate. None holds a state governor seat.

Bennett, the independent Maine candidate, is one of just 11 independent or third-party candidates vying for gubernatorial or US Senate seats whom Simmons expects to surpass the 5 percent vote threshold often used to gauge electoral viability.

Additionally, Simmons sees several ways independent congressional hopefuls could throw the midterm map into disarray, mainly in two key states: Nebraska and Montana.

He identified Nebraska’s independent Senate candidate Dan Osborn, a 51-year-old Navy veteran, as the “most likely alternative candidate to win” across the country, given the race has been deemed a toss-up by RealClearPolling, an election analysis website.

The Nebraska Democratic Party chose not to field a replacement nominee after its candidate withdrew, leaving Osborn as the principal challenger to Republican Pete Ricketts.

In Montana, Simmons sees another opening for an independent, though the race is complicated by the possibility of an unpredictable “spoiler” effect.

Seth Bodnar, a former University of Montana president and Army veteran, is running against Republican Kurt Alme for the US Senate seat. He is being backed by the Independent Leadership Council, which is also supporting California Congressman Kevin Kiley, who left the Republican Party to seek re-election as an independent.

Democratic US Senate candidate Alani Bankhead could siphon off support from the same centre-left and anti-Republican pool of voters that Bodnar draws from, risking a three-way vote and potentially leaving the Republican to collect the spoils.

“Montana is the second most likely,” Simmons said, adding that “if that were to happen, the Republican Party would be having an extraordinarily bad night”.

In Idaho and South Dakota, independent candidates are not forecast to win, but they are expected to make Republicans pay for treating those seats as safe, diverting money, campaign time and attention to races Republicans may have otherwise taken for granted.

The greatest impact of independents may be the pressure they have historically brought to bear on the major parties to “implement a new policy or govern more effectively”, Simmons said.

#48. Parents' concerns to trigger urgent checks in A&E

Two-year-old Finlay Roberts died after spending hours in a crowded hospital corridor. A coroner raised concerns about a lack of basic observations done by busy staff.

A new early warning system is being introduced in hospital emergency departments in England to help busy NHS staff identify sick children who are dangerously deteriorating.

A high overall score means a child needs more urgent attention. Concerns from staff, parents, carers or the child themselves will trigger an immediate escalation of care.

It comes after Health Secretary Yvette Cooper promised to stop "corridor care" for sick children because of long waits for hospital beds.

The new National Paediatric Early Warning System (PEWS) asks staff to check, log and grade vital signs, such as blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen levels.

The initiative is designed to save lives ahead of winter, when pressure on the NHS is at its highest.

On the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg, Cooper recently talked about the case of two-year-old Finlay Roberts, who died in 2024 after spending hours in a hospital corridor before being discharged.

Cooper apologised to Finlay's parents on behalf of the NHS, saying: "No child should be treated in a corridor. No patient should be treated in a corridor."

Whittington Health NHS Trust, where Finlay waited for treatment, extended its condolences to the family and said it continued to experience periods of exceptional demand across emergency care and, at times, some patients may receive care in temporary clinical areas, including corridors.

A similar "early warning" system to identify acutely ill adults, including those with sepsis, is already in use in emergency and general NHS hospital wards.

More than 90 hospitals across England have been using the alert system in children's wards, with different age-appropriate assessments available for babies through to teenagers.

The NHS has already said it will expand Martha's Rule to all hospital emergency departments in England. The rule gives patients, families and carers the right to request a rapid review if they are worried that a patient's condition is getting worse and their concerns are not being responded to.

The initiative is named after Martha Mills, who died aged 13 in 2021 after developing blood poisoning, or sepsis, in hospital following a bike accident.

A coroner ruled that her family's concerns about her worsening condition were not listened to and she would probably have survived had she been moved to intensive care earlier.

Martha's parents Merope and Paul came up with the idea of the rule in a bid to change the culture that contributed to her death.

A&E units will have a dedicated phone number that can be called to trigger an urgent review of a patient's care.

Duncan Burton, chief nursing officer for England, said the new early warning system for children in emergency departments was an "important next step in improving care for children and young people across England for this winter - and beyond".

He added: "In fast-paced emergency departments, staff need to spot quickly when a child's condition is deteriorating.

"This standardised approach will help them recognise the warning signs, communicate concerns, and make timely decisions when every moment matters."

The early warning system for children, PEWS, is a collaboration between NHS England and the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health and the Royal College of Nursing.

#55. Antonelli - the boy 'born in a garage' who became world champion

Antonelli - the boy 'born in a garage' who became world champion

This is an updated version of an article first published in August 2026

Kimi Antonelli was 10 when his father Marco first realised the boy sitting on his lap, operating the steering and gears, might have an exceptional talent as a racing driver.

The pair were at the Adria circuit in north-east Italy, Marco with his Lamborghini sports car team, Kimi competing in a karting race.

It was the end of the day and Marco, a former driver himself, asked his son: "Do you want to try the Lamborghini?"

Marco strapped himself and Kimi into the car, belts extended around both. Dad was in charge of the throttle and brake, his son would control the rest.

"I couldn't see the track because he was in front of me with a helmet on," Marco tells BBC Sport. "I could only see beside me the marker panels before each corner - 100m, 50m.

"We started to drive, step by step, faster, faster, faster. In the end, I pushed like I was alone and I arrived at the braking point at the end of the straight, 320km/h, braked very hard and at the limit.

"The car had a lot of oversteer. Kimi controlled the oversteer perfectly, then I went on the throttle, Kimi finished the oversteer, changed gear, and went.

"I said: 'OK, stop, because it has now become dangerous.' But when I saw the video, 10 years old, on my leg, oversteer, everything perfect, I said: 'Maybe he has something special.'"

And now Kimi, who turned 20 only in August, has dominated F1 in only his second season, and become the youngest world champion in the sport's history.

This is the story of a boy his dad says was "born in a garage".

Kimi Antonelli used to enjoy playing on a motor racing simulator

"Kimi from 15 days old was in the garage and heard the sound of the engine and the smell of the exhaust," says Marco, who worked with his wife Veronica on their racing team business.

It was the Christmas holidays when Kimi was about three, and Marco had brought home a simulator he had bought for his guests to try out at the Bologna Motor Show.

"Each evening, Kimi wanted to drive," Marco remembers.

Just like seven years later in the Lamborghini, Kimi would sit on Marco's leg, the controls divided in the same way.

"I remember one night I started to fall asleep because I had been at work very early and we had a crash because I didn't brake when we arrived at the end of the straight," Marco says.

"And Kimi said: 'Dad, dad, no sleep, no sleep. I have to drive.'"

Marco Antonelli used to be "stressed" about his son's passion for motorsport

Marco Antonelli did not originally want his son to become a racing driver, because of his own struggles during his career.

"Sometimes you are fast but not in the right position to become a professional driver," he says.

In the 1990s, Marco Antonelli was on the cusp of becoming a works driver in the DTM touring car series, then a huge hit with audiences across Europe.

He tested for Alfa Romeo, but then the team boss left and the opportunity evaporated. Another chance came up with BMW, alongside Roberto Ravaglia and Steve Soper - stars at the time. Again, the same thing happened.

"I suffered a lot for it," Marco says. "And, of course, I was scared that my son [would] relive the same situation. At the beginning, I was very stressed about the passion my son had for motorsport."

Kimi tried a go-kart for the first time when he was five, and immediately showed an awareness of racing lines, braking points and car control.

The Antonellis were what Marco describes as "a normal family". They could not possibly afford to pay for Kimi to take part in all the categories on the way to Formula 1.

Marco decided he could fund Kimi through karting and a year of the basic single-seater category, Formula 4. After that, he figured he might be able to put him in one of his GT cars and become professional that way.

But Kimi immediately started to impress. Whatever category he entered, he was quick, and won.

His full name is Andrea Kimi Antonelli and he told the BBC last year that his close friends and family call him "Andrea". However, in F1 he is universally known as "Kimi"

By the time Kimi was 11, his name was buzzing around motor racing circles. Mercedes heard about him, and their driver academy scout Gwen Lagrue flew to Italy to check him out.

It was a season in which Antonelli had ended up driving a kart with less power than those of his rivals. Marco had told Kimi this would be good for him - he should think not about the result, but about how to improve, always push hard.

"Gwen was impressed about this condition of power and driving in this category that Kimi did," Marco says. "And maybe they understood that Kimi was a different child."

Now Kimi was a member of the Mercedes driver academy, Marco could stop worrying about money, and start focusing on helping his son achieve his full potential.

F1 team boss Toto Wolff (right) had his eye on Antonelli as a successor to Lewis Hamilton one day

Mercedes were not the only big names in F1 who had noticed Antonelli's promise.

"When he was in karting, you could already see he was one of the stand-out talents," says four-time world champion Max Verstappen, the only other teenager to win an F1 race.

At 15, Antonelli was ready to move up to cars, and, as a final preparation, did a couple of races in a karting category with gears. On his debut, with minimal pre-event testing, he took pole position. After that, he went to the world final, and stuck it on pole again.

This was the end of 2021, after which Antonelli moved full-time into cars. Through 2022, he swept the board in various championships in Formula 4, and the same happened in Formula Regional in 2023.

Antonelli celebrates winning the 2022 Italian Formula 4 championship

Antonelli won the 2023 Formula Regional European Championship (Freca)

Antonelli won the European Karting Championships in 2021

Antonelli celebrating victory in the 2023 Freca championship

By now, Mercedes team boss Toto Wolff was already thinking that Antonelli would be his next F1 driver, potentially a replacement for Lewis Hamilton. With the seven-time champion approaching 40, Wolff was planning on taking control of that generational transition.

So when it came time to negotiate a new contract with Hamilton in 2023, Wolff played hardball. Hamilton wanted to sign for at least two firm years, Wolff only one. In the end, they compromised on a contract for 2024 with an option for 2025.

Hamilton, unimpressed, decided to look at his options, and signed to join Ferrari in 2025, telling Wolff in late January 2024, before the start of that season.

Wolff immediately phoned Marco Antonelli, to tell him the "very big news".

"'We put Kimi in Formula 1,'" Marco remembers Wolff saying. "'What do you think?'

"I said: 'Oh, [it] is a difficult question you ask me. Of course I'm happy that you think about Kimi but are you sure he's ready?'"

Mercedes were at a management away day. Wolff called his senior engineers together to tell them about Hamilton's decision.

"We were reviewing options and trying to come to a fairly rapid conclusion," says trackside engineering director Andrew Shovlin.

"We were all keen to just go with Kimi, which we knew was a bit of a risk. But it was quite exciting and we thought, based on his career to date, he had a good chance of being very good. It seemed like a good challenge."

Antonelli, at the age of 15, was among a group of young drivers alongside Lewis Hamilton at the launch of the 2022 Mercedes F1 car

Antonelli had not even driven an F1 car when Wolff made the call. Mercedes put together a testing programme for him, 20-odd days in cars a couple of years old at tracks all over the world to prepare him for his F1 debut in 2025.

Shovlin and the engineers were immediately impressed.

"It was how rapidly he would get up to speed," Shovlin says. "What you normally see with drivers is they're just incrementally going a little bit quicker, braking a bit later.

"If you compare with other drivers we've seen in an F1 car for the first time, the best I'd ever seen until then was George [Russell]. You suddenly saw someone who was quite similar.

"With Kimi, he took a couple of runs to work out how the car behaved and then he'd be more or less on the limit.

"The other thing you can see is the fidelity of the car control, how they can sit with it right on the edge of breaking away from them on entry to a corner, but they can just control it in that condition.

"Equally, when you looked at the mistakes he was making, they were often errors of judgement or lack of experience. They weren't run-out-of-talent mistakes."

Antonelli made one of these at the 2024 Italian Grand Prix. Wolff decided to give him his first experience of an F1 weekend in first practice at his home race at Monza.

It was a spectacular debut - for both good and bad reasons.

Antonelli set the fastest time on his first lap. Then he went for another. He was flying, miles quicker than anyone up to that point, but then he crashed heavily at the final corner, Parabolica.

"No-one on earth could have got the car around the corner at the speed he was attempting," Shovlin says. "He clearly got into his head that he would try and do something special and it was an error of judgment."

Wolff took Antonelli aside, told him it was OK, and not to worry about it. But the crash had a significant impact on Antonelli.

Antonelli's F1 practice debut did not go how he would have liked

Wolff was very clear in public about managing expectations of Antonelli going into his debut season.

"I conditioned myself so strong that I said: 'There's going to be moments of brilliance and moments where you want to tear your hair out'," Wolff says.

There were stand-out moments, including pole for the sprint in Miami.

But the middle part of the season, through Europe, was a disappointment. A change to the rear suspension design made the car nervous and rocked his confidence. His pace was not impressive, and he made too many mistakes.

"We expected more highlights from him," Shovlin says.

Marco Antonelli explains: "Mercedes needed to have a good result after a period that was not like the Hamilton era. And, of course, needed two cars that always take points.

"Kimi felt this necessity. The crash in Monza was very tough for him to forget. It created on him a big pressure."

Wolff said the "moments of brilliance were there" but then there was "a spell of nine races that was really not good".

"Me, personally, I never doubted," says Wolff. "Other people doubted. In the team and externally."

A year on from his practice debut, Antonelli crashed again in Monza practice.

"This was the point where I said: 'Kimi, you've got to turn this around. This is just not good enough'," says Wolff. "He said: 'I know.'"

Marco Antonelli says he and his son talked a lot during this period and that Kimi "understood that everything can change".

"The period was bad but you can see light at the end of the tunnel," he says. "And step by step he started to take confidence."

Of the seven F1 race wins by a teenager, Antonelli has six of them. He turned 20 two days after the Dutch Grand Prix in August

Once F1 had left Europe in the autumn of last year, there was a shift.

"Suddenly, we felt like we were making really good progress," says Shovlin.

"There were signs towards the end of the year. I'm not saying any of us thought we'd see what we've seen so far this year, but that we had a decent F1 driver."

Antonelli has been rather more than that this year. He took the season by storm, and established a consistent supremacy over Russell.

In the best car in the field, Antonelli took six poles and six wins in the first 11 races, establishing a 50-point lead in the championship.

Mercedes lost their dominant form in the second half of the season in the face of challenges from first McLaren and then Red Bull.

But Antonelli remained a consistent threat at the front, and he killed off his Monza ghosts in the Italian Grand Prix with a stunning victory after an engine penalty led to him starting 19th on the grid.

He put in a drive of clinical excellence and, with help from a safety car, chased down and caught Russell in the final laps to take a win that will go down in folklore.

The extent of his turnaround in form has shocked almost everyone.

"We were all surprised," Wolff says. "My thoughts were always that he needed to make the experience of knowing all the tracks, the pressure of performing on such a high level under the spotlight of the media, the constant public feedback, whether you're doing well or not, the situations everyone wanted to be close to him.

"There wasn't one single defining factor where I would have said that made it difficult for him, but this year, he knew exactly what to expect. What was really nice to see is that the self-confidence, without feeling any entitlement, has shone."

Marco points to his son's "very strong" mentality.

"In F4 and Freca, he started with not good results, and he was able to remake everything and, at the end, work hard and win the championship," he says.

"We talk about F1. I don't know what the spark is in his brain, but Kimi in the past did the same."

Verstappen says: "For me, there was never any doubt that he would make the jump the year after and already be a lot more consistently competitive.

"It's normal in the progression of a driver. In your rookie season, it can be quite tough, but at the moment, of course, he's doing an amazing job."

Kimi pays tribute to his father, describing him as his "rock", while Wolff also praises the driver's "typical Italian family".

"They are caring, they are loving," Wolff says. "Kimi's character, he's gentle, he has empathy, he has charisma, he has the right amount of intelligence for being a racing driver."

Antonelli describes his dad as his "rock"

Mercedes have been talking to Antonelli about managing his emotions, which have flared at times this season, such as in the sprint in Canada, when the racing between the two Mercedes drivers came close to boiling over.

Shovlin says: "He just says: 'I'm young, I'm Italian, what do you expect?'"

The remark reveals Antonelli's sense of humour, but also a maturity and self-awareness that might not always be expected in someone so young.

Wolff says: "He has all that. He's actually not [fitting the stereotype of] a typical emotional Italian. He's so clear [in] what he needs to do in a car. I don't see lots of emotion. I see full focus, full concentration. I see, 'OK, this is a problem, let's analyse it, and move on.'

"You see him in a debrief afterwards. You couldn't really say whether there was a bad or a good session. It's all very balanced and stable."

Antonelli took advice on this when he met tennis legend Roger Federer at Wimbledon this summer.

"We chatted about my races," Antonelli says. "About when he used to play, and about life in general.

"About pressure, he just told me to focus on one race at a time, just focus on what you can control, and also to control the emotions, especially the ones that can make you do mistakes."

Federer's words echoed something Marco has always impressed on his son.

"In our family, we use this concept in normal life as well," he says.

"We have to live every day our best day. You work hard every day to arrive at the end of the project you have."

Back in February 2024, Wolff followed up his phone call to Marco with a meeting in Milan.

"Toto said 'Of course it will be difficult, but I think after one year of experience, we can do very well with Kimi in F1'," Marco says.

"The project was 2025 experience, 2026 start to achieve results. I think we overachieved."

#58. Zverev becomes deserved world number one - but sense of discomfort remains

Alexander Zverev spent 13 years waiting to win a Grand Slam title - then won two in three months

Before this year, Alexander Zverev was, for many, the best male player never to have won a Grand Slam.

He will end 2026 as a two-time major champion and deserved world number one.

The 29-year-old has become the oldest man in more than 50 years to become world number one for the first time, with victories at the French Open and US Open providing the springboard.

Diagnosed with type 1 diabetes aged four, his overdue Grand Slam breakthrough was a reward for persistence and intense hard work.

However, the new standard-bearer for men's tennis has been accused of physical abuse by two former partners, and been the subject of a major independent investigation by the sport.

At the start of this year, Zverev was already an Olympic champion, two-time winner of the season-ending ATP Finals and a former world number two who had appeared in three Grand Slam finals.

The most excruciating of those was the 2020 US Open final, in which he led Dominic Thiem by two sets to love but lost in a fifth-set tie-break after serving two double faults as his opponent began to cramp.

The inability to play with the same freedom and attacking intent that is often on show in big matches on the ATP Tour had become a perennial problem.

This year would be different, he promised himself. He would hit the ball with more speed, take greater risk and employ more variety in rallies.

The impetus stemmed from the 10 days he spent with Rafael and Toni Nadal in Mallorca in July 2025.

"I wanted to see and then hear a new vision of what tennis in their mind looks like - and how my tennis, in their mind, looks like," Zverev explained.

"Rafa, for example, played against me for 10 years. So now, as a spectator and also as an opponent, he can probably give you the best advice anybody can, because he knows what it's like to be on the court with me."

There were still moments of extreme anxiety, including cramping in June's French Open final against Flavio Cobolli.

But this time Zverev made no mistake in the deciding set, winning it 6-1 to claim his long-awaited first major trophy.

Mischa Zverev (left) also played on the ATP Tour, with Alexander following his older brother around as a child

Zverev's on-court success is a family affair. His father, Alex Sr, has been a near-constant presence in the coaching box, while older brother Mischa has joined him since his retirement from playing.

That particular relationship has come full circle, as Zverev was once a toddler sitting by the side of the court as Mischa practised with Andy Murray as a junior.

Zverev has said his relationship with his father works so well as they spend "zero time together" off court.

"The big difference with my parents is that they never forced me to do anything," Zverev once recalled.

"They wanted me to play different sports so they didn't have to coach me. They always told me 'why don't you play football, or field hockey?' as that was the sport I loved.

"They are very smart. They've been through it. They've been tennis players themselves."

Not only is Zverev now the French and US Open champion, but he was also runner-up at Wimbledon, where he played his best tennis of the year.

He was aggressive and daring in a four-set defeat by Jannik Sinner on Centre Court, and will need to take a leaf out of the same book in future meetings with both the Italian and Carlos Alcaraz.

The German has lost 10 matches in a row to Sinner, and if he and Alcaraz are fit for the start of next season, Zverev's challenge will be to make 2027 a three-way rivalry for the major titles.

Zverev has previously been the subject of serious allegations.

In October 2020, his ex-partner Olga Sharypova accused him of physical violence and controlling behaviour.

Zverev maintained his innocence from the start and described the allegations as baseless.

There were no legal proceedings, but the ATP Tour opened a major investigation that was carried out by third-party investigators.

It found "insufficient evidence to substantiate published allegations of abuse".

"Based on a lack of reliable evidence and eyewitness reports, in addition to conflicting statements by Sharypova, Zverev and other interviewees, the investigation was unable to substantiate the allegations of abuse," the ATP said in January 2023.

A director of safeguarding was subsequently appointed, and this year a new safeguarding programme was launched to introduce a "clear, global framework for preventing and responding to abuse, including cases of harassment, bullying, domestic violence and other forms of misconduct".

In October 2023, Zverev was issued with a penalty order and fined 450,000 euros (Â£392,000) by a German court for bodily harm against another ex-girlfriend, Brenda Patea.

Zverev denied the claims and contested the order, with a public trial taking place in Berlin in May 2024.

A week into the process, the court terminated the proceedings before a verdict could be reached, and told the BBC there had been a settlement between Zverev and Patea.

"The decision is not a verdict and it is not a decision about guilt or innocence," the court said.

"One decisive factor for the court decision was that the witness [Zverev's ex-partner] has expressed her wish to end the trial."

Zverev reached the final of three of the four Grand Slams in 2026

Zverev reached the French Open final on the day the settlement was announced. Having spoken earlier in the tournament about the trial being a chance to prove his innocence, he was asked whether he had any regrets about settling before that was possible.

"That's what dropping the case is. That is innocence," he argued.

"They're not going to drop the case if you're guilty at the end of the day. I don't know what translations you have, but that's what it means.

"Done. We move on. I never ever want to hear another question about the subject again. That goes out to everybody."

Patea has not publicly commented on the settlement.

Zverev was heckled by spectators in 2025 at the Australian Open and Munich Open over the allegations.

In 2022, he was given an eight-week suspended ban by the ATP Tour after being found guilty of a 'major offence' for smashing his racquet into the umpire's chair four times after losing a doubles match at the Mexican Open.

Zverev, who was thrown out of the tournament in Acapulco for unsportsmanlike behaviour, was incensed by a line call that went against him and partner Marcelo Melo in a deciding tie-break.

He swore at umpire Alessandro Germani and accused him of destroying the whole match. One of his four angry swipes at the chair nearly caught the umpire on the foot.

Zverev later apologised and said it was "difficult to put into words" how much he regretted his behaviour.

The ban was never imposed, as Zverev avoided being fined for 'disrespectful or aggressive behaviour, or verbal or physical abuse' in the 12 months following the incident.

Josh Hazlewood, Pat Cummins and Mitchell Starc won the 2023 ODI World Cup with Australia

Australia have omitted the star pace trio of Pat Cummins, Josh Hazlewood and Mitchell Starc from their squads for the upcoming white-ball series against England.

The veteran seamers played all three Tests in the recent 2-1 series defeat by South Africa and have been rested for the three-match ODI series that starts in Perth on 13 November.

Cummins and Hazlewood have also been left out for the five-match T20 series that begins on 21 November, while Starc, 36, has already retired from the format.

Fast bowler Mahli Beardman, 21, has been named in both squads and could make his international debut in ODIs, while the uncapped pair of Sam Harper and Lachlan Shaw have been included for the T20s.

Alex Carey, Cooper Connolly, Cameron Green, Travis Head, Josh Inglis and Matt Renshaw, who also played in the South Africa series, are included for the ODIs but do not feature in the T20 squad.

Although Cummins, 33, formally remains the ODI captain, he has only played one 50-over fixture since November 2024 and Mitchell Marsh, his frequent deputy and T20 skipper, will step in again for the longer format.

Seamer Nathan Ellis, a white-ball regular, is only included in the T20 squad after suffering a side strain in the September ODI tour of South Africa and Zimbabwe, while fellow pacer Jack Edwards is named in both squads after making his debut on that trip.

All-rounder Liam Scott, who played one 50-over match against Bangladesh in June, is recalled to the ODI squad, while spinners Nikhil Chaudhary and Joel Davies, who both made their only T20 appearances on the same tour, are selected for the 20-over fixtures.

Top-order batters Jake Fraser-McGurk and Mitchell Owen are recalled to the T20 squads after not featuring in recent series, while Tim David is only selected for the shorter format despite a recent return to 50-over domestic cricket.

Following the final England T20 on 2 December, Australia will start a heavy pre-Ashes Test schedule in which Cummins, Starc and Hazlewood, 35, will be expected to feature frequently.

They begin a four-match home Test series against New Zealand on 9 December before travelling for a five-Test series against India that starts on 21 January.

They then face England in a one-off Test at the Melbourne Cricket Ground from 11 March, celebrating 150 years of the Ashes, prior to the Indian Premier League.

Australia could also conceivably reach the World Test Championship final at the Oval on 9 June, shortly before the five-game Ashes series that runs from 18 June to 2 August.

The ODI World Cup in South Africa, Namibia and Zimbabwe will then begin on 7 October.
