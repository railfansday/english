#1. Two questions that prevent homebuilder growth mistakes

In a book excerpt analysis, Scott Cox recounts being hired to find deals, then fired after advising a builder to shrink. The takeaway is to align on 2 inputs before growth, risk tolerance and market return assumptions.  AI Summary

[Editor’s note: HousingWire The Builder’s Daily contributing author Scott Cox has just published Less Wrong, More Right: Judgment, Risk, and the Questions That Actually Matter in Homebuilding and Land Development, a new book exploring how the decisions that shape homebuilding businesses, land investments, and careers often hinge less on having the right answers than on asking the right questions.

In the first of seven excerpts we’re pleased to share with TBD readers, Cox draws on a hard-earned personal lesson to illustrate a central argument of the book: Experience, expertise and abundant information are no substitute for identifying the question that matters most – before the consequences of missing it become clear.]

I had been the banker for a builder who was once among the larger operators in their market and had come out the other side of a rough stretch considerably smaller. The owner wanted to grow the company back to what it had been, and asked whether I would be interested in joining him. Of course I would.

I went looking for deals. I found some. He said no. I found more. He said no to those. I asked what he was looking for and got an answer that amounted to better. More months, more no’s. Eventually I went back and said: I know I’m inexperienced at this, and if I’m looking in the wrong places, I need direction. He had a few suggestions. None of them changed what was available.

I told him what I had concluded, which was that we either did deals that looked like these or he should consider shrinking the company dramatically. He had an extraordinary land position — a ranch, acquired under a structure nobody could replicate — and he could build out on it for years with a much smaller team. He did not need me for that.

Some weeks later he came to me and said he had good news and bad news. The good news was that he had talked to several people he trusted and they all told him I had given him excellent advice. The bad news was that he intended to take it. I was fired.

He shrank the company, built out the ranch, and did very well.

At the time I was fully occupied with the pressing question of what I was going to do next. Later — thinking through what happened and what I might have done differently to see this coming — I worked out what I could have done differently. There was exactly one thing, and it had nothing to do with how I performed the job. It was two questions I never asked before I said yes.

The first was what are you prepared to do, and to risk, in order to grow? I was financing enough deals then to know that market returns were modest. Growing meant doing those deals and signing personal guarantees to get them done. He was not willing to. He had been willing once, at the beginning, and he was not willing now.

He answered it himself, sometime after I had taken the job. We were driving somewhere together, and it occurred to me I had never once asked him how he got started. He laughed and said that if he had had any idea how hard it would be, he would not have done it.

I laughed too. I took it for exactly what it sounded like – a rueful war story about how hard the first build had been. No alarm bell rang. I had just been handed the answer to that first question, in his own voice, and I heard a punch line. I have seen it enough times since – the founder who did it once and thinks they would like to again, but forgot what it was like and the risks they took to accomplish it. Warren Buffett famously stated, “It’s insane to risk what you have and need for something you don’t really need”. But it’s not a mindset that builds companies from the ground up.

The second was here is what I believe market returns are; do you agree? We never established that we were looking at the same market. I assumed we were, because we were in the same business, in the same market, in the same year.

Maybe he would have said yes to both and we would have arrived in the same place anyway. People do not always know their own minds until they have to actually sign up for the risk – when the blue pen is in their hand. But I never made him address the questions. I missed the questions the entire arrangement was balanced on.

About 10 years later, I had my final interview with the CEO of a public builder, for a division president role. This time I asked the second question. What do you think sustainable returns in that market actually are? He thought they were better than I did – by a wide margin. Literally and figuratively. I called up the regional president who wanted me for the role and said I would pass. The division was closed a few years later after it disappointed.

The reality was that I could have done a solid job. I knew the market, I had enough people in it who would have helped me, and the builder had plenty of capital. None of that was going to determine whether it worked. What was going to determine whether it worked was what the market handed us over the next several years — and he and I did not agree about what that was. That was the bet. That was the question.

That is not a story about one bad job. It is the shape of nearly every expensive mistake I have watched, and most of the ones I have made. The expensive mistakes in this business are not usually made by people who don’t know enough. They are made by people who know a great deal, who have gathered more than enough data, but either missed or underweighted the biggest question.

This is the throughline and argument of the book. A vague question produces information. A great question produces knowledge. They are not the same thing, and one does not accumulate into the other. You can gather information indefinitely – reports, studies, comps, dashboards, a data room with everything in it – and end up not knowing what actually matters the most. A specific, well-built question does something different. It forces you to arrange what you have so that a useful answer can appear at all.

I have tried to use both positive and negative examples, but the book leans toward the ones that did not go well. That should not suggest that I, my firms, our partners, or the people I have worked alongside had more mistakes than successes. We would not all still be here if that were true. It is that we learn more from the mistakes. The lessons sink in harder when they arrive with some pain attached. There are a couple of sayings that make the point. Wounds heal, scars last. And anything that happened to somebody else is a theory; if it happened to you, it is a fact. I hope some of these land well enough to be taken as more than theory.

#3. Builder Sentiment Falls to 14-Year Low in October

Builder sentiment dropped to a 14-year low in October due to rising mortgage rates, elevated energy and material costs, and worsening labor and lot conditions.

Builder confidence in the market for newly built single-family homes fell three points to 29 in October, according to the latest NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI). The reading is the lowest HMI value since June 2012.

The HMI has remained below 40 for 18 consecutive months. An index value below 50 indicates more builders view conditions as poor than good. In addition to the overall index, each of the three component indices registered readings below 40 in October.

“Mortgage rates have climbed well above 7% in recent weeks, and higher gas prices and inflation have further weakened buyer traffic across much of the country,” said NAHB chairman Bill Owens. “Though builders are also contending with higher costs for construction loans, building materials, and diesel, the Trump administration’s efforts to advance regulatory reform are helping to ease some of the cost pressures they face.”

Owens noted that one-third of builders reported immigration enforcement activity in their market areas during the past month, another pressure on the labor market causing construction delays and discouraging legal workers from reporting to work.

The HMI mirrors results from Zonda’s monthly survey of builders. Zonda’s most recent builder survey found 84% of builders reported slow demand with 60% indicating the slow demand is “causing concern.” Anecdotally, one surveyed builder said the fall housing market was the worst it had experienced since 2007.

“Housing remains squeezed by supply-side constraints and significant affordability challenges,” said NAHB chief economist Robert Dietz. “Demand persists, but higher mortgage rates, elevated construction costs, and weaker consumer confidence are keeping more buyers on the sideline. As a result, buyer traffic has fallen to its lowest level since April 2012, excluding the pandemic’s onset in April 2020.”

To illustrate the impacts of affordability pressures in the market, the October HMI found 43% of builders cut prices during the month, the highest share in the past six years. Additionally, 65% of builders reported using sales incentives, the 19th straight month the share has been at or above 60%.

The HMI is composed of three indices gauging builder perceptions of current sales conditions, sales expectations for the next six months, and traffic of prospective buyers.

The HMI index measuring current sales conditions fell two points to 33, the metric tracking future sales expectations dropped five points to 32, and the index gauging prospective buyer traffic fell five points to 18.

On a regional basis, the three-month moving average HMI scores fell in all four Census regions.

#4. The borrowers traditional underwriting wasn’t built for

Upwork reports 76 million Americans participate in freelance work, and many borrowers now have income outside W-2 paychecks. The column argues underwriting should incorporate cash flow, deposits and business performance, pairing analytics with experienced human judgment.   AI Summary

Mortgage lending has spent the last several years focused on interest rates, affordability and, more recently, artificial intelligence. Those are important conversations. But another shift may prove just as significant over the next decade: More Americans are earning income outside the traditional paycheck, while much of mortgage underwriting still assumes they don’t. The core issue is straightforward: Underwriting must evolve to reflect how people earn income. That mismatch is becoming impossible to ignore.

According to Upwork’s 2026 Future Workforce Index, more than 76 million Americans now participate in freelance work, and roughly half of Gen Z workers already earn at least part of their income independently. For millions of future homebuyers, freelancing isn’t a side hustle, it’s simply how they earn a living. That shift is reshaping the future homebuyer, and underwriting needs to evolve with it.

Even the data on who these buyers are varies. The National Association of Realtors’ most recent survey puts the typical first-time homebuyer at a record age of 40, while the Mortgage Bankers Association, using loan-level data, estimates the figure closer to 32. Regardless of where the exact number lands, today’s buyers look dramatically different than they did just a decade ago, yet many underwriting practices haven’t evolved at the same pace.

Traditional underwriting was built around predictable paychecks and W-2 income. While that approach continues to work well for many borrowers, it frequently struggles to accurately evaluate entrepreneurs, independent contractors, consultants and small business owners whose earnings don’t fit neatly into a standard formula. This is the difference between traditional models and today’s income reality.

Tax returns tell an important story, but they don’t always tell the complete one. A successful marketing consultant billing well into six figures may appear to earn far less after legitimate business deductions.

A growing business owner may be evaluated based on income over two years, even when the business has experienced significant recent growth. In many cases, these borrowers earn more than applicants with traditional W-2 jobs yet appear weaker on paper because the underwriting model wasn’t designed for how they generate income.

The encouraging news is that lenders don’t have to choose between responsible lending and serving these borrowers well. Today’s market includes underwriting approaches that evaluate cash flow, business performance, assets and deposit history in ways that often provide a more complete picture of a borrower’s financial strength. The opportunity is knowing when they are appropriate and providing borrowers with informed guidance.

Technology also has an increasingly important role to play. Artificial intelligence and advanced analytics can review financial records in minutes, identify income patterns and surface insights that previously required hours of manual analysis. These tools are making the mortgage process faster and more efficient.

But technology still has limits. AI can analyze documents. It can organize data. It can accelerate workflows. What cannot yet be replaced is the judgment required to understand how a business owner earns a living or explain that financial story in a way that supports sound underwriting decisions. Human expertise remains essential, particularly when evaluating borrowers whose income falls outside traditional models.

That may be the industry’s greatest opportunity. Loan officers who understand non-traditional income can identify opportunities others overlook, ask better questions and help qualified borrowers avoid unnecessary denials before an application is ever submitted. As the workforce evolves, that knowledge becomes a valuable competitive advantage.

Equally important is access to experienced in-house underwriters. Complex income rarely fits neatly inside a checklist. Direct collaboration between loan officers and underwriters often uncovers solutions that would be difficult to identify through layers of outsourced review. Better collaboration frequently leads to better outcomes for qualified borrowers while preserving appropriate underwriting standards.

The mortgage industry has always adapted to changes in the housing market. Now it must adapt to changes in the workforce.

The definition of a qualified borrower is evolving. That’s not because responsible lending standards are changing, but because the way Americans earn income is changing. Lenders that recognize this shift, invest in education, embrace technology thoughtfully and equip their teams to evaluate borrowers more completely will be better prepared to serve the next generation of homeowners.

The others may find that some of tomorrow’s most qualified borrowers no longer fit yesterday’s definition of income.

Our job isn’t to lower the bar, but instead, to become better at recognizing qualified borrowers whose financial lives don’t fit yesterday’s definitions.

#5. Why You Can’t Take Your Low Mortgage Rate to a New Home

With mortgage rates on a brutal climb, Congress has once again brought up the idea of creating portable mortgages.

The idea might sound good, but it could have huge implications for the U.S. housing market and would be more difficult to implement than it may seem.

A portable mortgage would allow you to take your existing mortgage—and its rate—with you when you move to a new home. Congressional leaders proposed the idea a few times as a solution to rising housing costs. But experts say things would get complicated and quickly.

Also, the idea might not bring the affordability relief that leaders hope for. Realtor.com® senior economist Jake Krimmel says it could actually make the market less affordable.

"Investors of mortgage-backed securities in a world of portable mortgages would demand compensation for that uncertainty around a different pre-payment risk schedule as well as the new unpriced risk that comes from porting a mortgage to a new home," Krimmel says. "And those latter two new risks could translate into higher mortgage rates."

The latest bill in Congress to create a portable mortgage, the Making Ownership Viable for Everyone, or MOVE Act, was introduced by Rep. Thomas Kean (R-NJ) in June. Since then, housing affordability has only worsened as mortgage rates climbed.

Last week, the average rate on 30-year fixed home loans rose to 7.40%, according to Freddie Mac. That's the highest it's been since 2023. The year has been marked by an upward surge in rates driven by inflation fears, a global bond sell-off, and rising concerns about government deficits.

With a portable mortgage, you wouldn't have to buy a new mortgage when you buy a new home. That means those who locked in low rates when they bought homes a few years ago could keep them. Those low rates, experts think, discourage many would-be sellers from entering the current market environment. Instead, in theory, they'd be able to take the loan, rates, and terms with them to a new home.

The problem is that the math gets very complicated very quickly. If you move to a more expensive home, you'd have to pay down the difference in price, or get a second loan just to cover that amount. And the system would face far larger problems, too, Krimmel says.

"Feasibility is the whole game when it comes to portable mortgages," Krimmel explains. "The U.S. mortgage market is built on securitization, where investors buy pools of loans priced against the specific homes behind them.

"A portable mortgage breaks that link by decoupling the loan from the home it was originated against," Krimmel continues. "It also removes the loan payoff that happens when a homeowner sells (called pre-payment), which makes the loans much harder to value in a securitized market."

The U.K. and Canada both allow that. But their markets are different, Krimmel says. That's because their systems don't have built-in 30-year fixed-rate mortgages. Rates reset every few years and mortgage durations are shorter. And loans have pre-payment penalties.

As a result, loans aren't securitized and more often stay on bank balence sheets.

The other idea sometimes floated is an assumable mortgage. In an assumable loan, a homebuyer can step into a seller's loan. Those are available in some programs, including FHA, VA, and USDA loans.

Realtor.com estimates about 10% of active listings today are potential candidates for assumability. But it's hard to tell how many actually are. Only about 0.6% of listings mention "assumable" in the description. Krimmel says this model, too, faces an uphill battle to wider acceptance in the U.S. housing market.

"Again, it's all about prepayment expectations and securitization," Krimmel says. "Investors in mortgage-backed securities get repaid when a home sells because conventional loans come due when the home sells. If you break that link between sale and the loan coming due, that changes pre-payment expectations, the securitization market, and likely mortgage rates (via spreads) as a result."

Former Federal Housing Administration Commissioner Frank Cassidy tells Realtor.com there could be broader impacts on the secondary market if portable or assumable mortgages became available.

"It significantly affects the attractiveness of the mortgage-backed security and in turn can affect the interest rate," Cassidy says. "So it may solve one problem in order to be able to hold your mortgage longer. But the pricing may look much different."

Federal Housing Finance Agency Director Bill Pulte said last year the Trump administration was considering portable mortgages along with a few other ideas. It also suggested a 50-year mortgage but backed away from the idea.

Kean's Making Ownership Viable for Everyone Act has no co-sponsors and is stuck in committee. It would direct Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to begin purchasing portable mortgages.

“I have heard directly from residents across our state who feel stuck in homes that no longer meet their needs because moving would mean giving up their mortgage rate," Kean said in a statement. "I wrote this bill, the MOVE Act, to give homeowners more freedom, help put more homes on the market, and make the American Dream of owning a home more attainable for families across New Jersey.”

Congress most recently discussed the idea in a House Financial Services hearing in February. In it, leaders also expressed some skepticism about the feasibility of changing the mortgage system so drastically.

"If you wanted to have a portable mortgage, that would be really hard for an existing mortgage that's been sold in the secondary market, wouldn't it?" Rep. French Hill (R-AR) asked Norbert Michel, director of the Center for Monetary and Financial Alternatives for the Cato Institute.

"It would be a contract violation," Michel responded.

#6. Home Sellers Now Outnumber Buyers By 63%, the Biggest Gap Since the Post-Recession Housing Crash

Homebuyers have more negotiating power than they’ve had since the housing market crashed in the aftermath of the Great Recession. There were an estimated 62.6% more home sellers than buyers in the U.S. housing market in September, the biggest gap in records dating back to 2013.

That marks the fourth record-breaking month in a row, and the margin is up sharply from 56.9% in August. The Sun Belt is driving the extreme buyer’s market nationwide; led by huge seller surpluses in Miami, Nashville, TN, and a handful of Texas metros.

When sellers outnumber buyers, buyers typically have more negotiating power because they have options. That’s why a market with a lot more sellers than buyers is considered a buyer’s market. We define a market where there are over 10% more sellers than buyers as a buyer’s market and a market where there are over 10% fewer sellers than buyers as a seller’s market. A market where the gap is plus or minus 10% is considered a balanced market.

We estimated the number of buyers using proprietary Redfin data on the typical time from a buyer’s first tour to close of purchase, and MLS data on active listings and pending sales. The estimated number of sellers in the market is simply the number of active listings in the MLS. These estimates are seasonally adjusted and subject to revision. See a more detailed methodology here and view an interactive dashboard here.

It’s only a buyer’s market for people who can afford to buy. High housing costs and widespread economic uncertainty have caused many would-be buyers to back off in recent years, creating the imbalance of buyers and sellers we see today.

“This is a buyer’s market born out of caution, not strength,” said Asad Khan, a senior economist at Redfin. “Buyers have tremendous negotiating power in most of the country because so many other would-be buyers have been priced out of the market, or scared off by economic uncertainty. For house hunters who have the budget and job security to purchase a home in today’s market, that creates a unique opportunity: They can be picky, negotiate and even walk away if the deal doesn’t make sense financially or they find something they like better. The irony is that in terms of supply, these are some of the best conditions for buyers in many years–but they have arrived at a time when costs are so high that fewer people can afford to take advantage of them.”

The gap between buyers and sellers widened in September because buyers pulled back, while sellers kept showing up.

There were an estimated 928,892 homebuyers in the market nationwide in September, the lowest level in records dating back through 2013. That’s down 2.4% from a month earlier, the fifth straight monthly decline.

On the flip side, there were an estimated 1,510,784 home sellers in the market, the most since March 2020. That’s up 1.2% month over month.

The number of homes on the market is rising largely because many homes are lingering without finding buyers. That builds up a big pool of listings, and each month, new listings add to that pool.

Homeowners have continued listing despite falling demand because many have simply given up waiting. After years on the sidelines hoping rates would drop or demand would rebound, some sellers see little reason to keep waiting, especially with prices still elevated enough to sell at a gain. Some homeowners are choosing to sell now because they’re concerned prices could drop if they wait longer. Others are listing out of necessity: new jobs, growing families, divorce or retirement, for example. But note that many homeowners who don’t need to move may decide not to list, or pull their homes off the market: Many have substantial equity, and the relatively strong job market means there’s typically not much pressure to sell.

Over 80% of U.S. metro areas—41 of the 50 U.S. metro areas Redfin analyzed—are buyer’s markets. That’s the most in records dating back through 2013.

For the second month in a row, Miami, Nashville and Houston have the biggest surpluses of sellers over buyers. In the top 13 buyer’s markets, there were more than twice as many sellers as buyers in September. All of them are located in the Sun Belt; four are in Texas and four are in Florida.

The Sun Belt—particularly Texas and Florida—is home to some of the nation’s biggest buyer’s markets partly because homebuilding has been so robust. Builders continue to add newly constructed homes even as buyer demand has cooled, contributing to a buildup of inventory and giving buyers more options and negotiating power.

Demand has cooled largely because buyers are increasingly priced out, with mortgage rates rising and home prices holding up. And in Florida, other costs associated with homebuying are rising, too, including HOA and insurance fees.

Four metros were “balanced” in September—neither buyer’s nor seller’s markets. Two New England metros are trending toward becoming buyer’s markets: Boston, where sellers outnumbered buyers by 7.4% after being roughly even the month before, and Providence, RI, where sellers outnumbered buyers by 2.4%, a reversal from the prior month, when there were 7.4% fewer sellers than buyers.

The surplus of home sellers over buyers grew month over month in all but two of the buyer’s markets in the nation in September.

The surplus increased most in Miami, which  also has the biggest surplus of sellers over buyers; there were 158.9% more sellers than buyers in September, up from 134.5% the month before. The next-biggest monthly increase was in Tampa, where there were 103.7% more sellers than buyers in September, up from 82.2% in August. Warren, MI rounds out the top three (47.1% more sellers than buyers, up from 27.3%).

“Sometimes, when buyer demand drops, seller activity drops right along with it—so the overall balance of power barely budges,” Khan said. “But that’s not what we’re seeing this month. In every single metro where the number of buyers fell from the month before, the gap between sellers and buyers increased, causing the market to tilt further in buyers’ favor. That’s because sellers didn’t pull back nearly as fast, or pull back at all.”

House hunters lost negotiating power in just two buyer’s markets. The surplus of sellers over buyers fell from August to September in St. Louis and Detroit, both of which are tilted in buyers’ favor, but not nearly as much as the Sun Belt metros. In Detroit, there were 37.9% more sellers than buyers in September, down from 46.5% the month before. In St. Louis, it was 24.5%, down from 26.9%.

Just five of the major U.S. metro areas Redfin analyzed were seller’s markets in September.

Nassau County, NY was the number-one seller’s market, with 28.6% fewer sellers than buyers. The other seller’s markets were Montgomery County, PA (-22.7%), Newark, NJ (-18.5%), Milwaukee (-16.1%) and San Francisco (-10.6%). That’s the second month in a row those five were the only seller’s markets in the nation.

The seller’s markets are concentrated in places where limited homebuilding has kept housing supply tight. In the greater New York City area, years of constrained construction, combined with strong demand for homes near a major job center, have left buyers competing for relatively few listings. San Francisco is an exception: The housing market has heated up as the AI boom creates wealth, fueling an increase in home sales and competition among buyers.

Home-sale prices rose an average of 6.4% year over year across the five seller’s markets in August. That’s compared with a 1% uptick across all of the buyer’s markets—a sign that in seller’s markets, competition among buyers is pushing up home prices.

#7. Kitchen Design Is Changing Direction, Designers Say

The kitchen is getting a functional makeover, according to the National Kitchen and Bath Association’s (NKBA) 2027 Kitchen Trends Report.

Fully open spaces are falling out of favor, for a balanced, more practical blend of open and closed spaces, which is changing everything in the kitchen from layout to adjacent living spaces to storage solutions.

This is remarkable, because it reverses the NKBA’s position on kitchen design from only a year ago, which identified open-concept as still in a growth phase.

Defined zones, delineated by partial walls, and operable enclosures are rising in popularity, and boldly, “intentional zoning” is rated here to stay by more than 80% of designers.

Designers named semi-enclosed layouts (46%) and partially concealed kitchens (41%) as up-and-coming features to watch; 64% say open shelving is out, while almost half expect the return of the hardworking back kitchen.

Bill Darcy, global president & CEO of NKBA, says, “While the kitchen has always been the ‘heart of the home,’ that idea has taken on new meaning. From mudrooms and laundry rooms to back pantries and personalized work zones, the report shows that we’ve entered a new era of what the kitchen can do for homeowners—solving real problems, adding home value, and elevating the way we live.”

Although the about-face on open-concept is significant, it’s not entirely surprising, given how pragmatic homeowners are becoming in their design choices. Flexibility between open and closed spaces gives more multifunctional options, especially important as homes scale down in size.

The kitchen is increasingly the hub of a functional ecosystem that supports daily living, including the laundry and mudrooms. Ventilation, water filtration, touchless faucets, and sound-absorbing materials, for a more peaceful kitchen, are all gaining in popularity.

Meanwhile, client resilience matters too. Designers say to watch for increased interest in leak detection systems, shut-off valves, and backup power.

#8. Mortgage needs a new productivity metric: Human attention per loan

Mortgage workflows still consume hours of human attention despite more tools. A proposed productivity metric, human attention minutes per loan, can better show whether AI reduces rework, exceptions and cost.   AI Summary

A mortgage can move through a POS, LOS, AUS, pricing engine, document platform and several workflow tools and still consume hours of human attention.

The industry has spent years improving individual steps in the mortgage process. Yet the economics remain difficult. According to the Mortgage Bankers Association, total loan production expenses averaged $10,936 per loan in the second quarter of 2026. That was an improvement from the first quarter, but still well above the historical average of $7,945.

We measure cost per loan, cycle time, pull-through, defects and loans per employee. All of those matter. But as AI begins to move deeper into mortgage operations, I think we are missing one important measure:

How much human attention does it take to complete a loan? Not headcount. Not touches for the sake of counting touches. Actual human attention.

A processor may spend a few minutes finding the right document, another few reconciling two values, then time checking whether a condition was satisfied. An underwriter may reopen documents because the information presented to them is incomplete or not trusted. A QC reviewer may repeat checks already performed upstream because the evidence behind the earlier decision is difficult to reconstruct.

None of these activities looks particularly expensive in isolation. Across thousands of loans, they become the operating model.

This is why simply adding AI to existing workflows may not materially change the economics. If AI extracts information but a person still validates every field, we may have improved extraction without reducing work. If a model produces 30 findings and a reviewer must investigate 25 of them to discover that only three matter, we have created another queue.

The better question is not, “How much can AI do?” It is, “What work still deserves human attention after AI has done what it can reliably do?”

Most mortgage operational work can be thought of in three buckets.

The first is repeatable work: classifying documents, identifying missing items, extracting information, comparing values, checking dates and completing deterministic validations. Increasingly, technology should perform this work without requiring a person to confirm every successful step.

The second is exception work. Something is missing, inconsistent, ambiguous or outside a defined tolerance. This is where technology should narrow the problem before handing it to a person. A reviewer should see the issue, the relevant evidence and why it was surfaced, rather than starting again with the entire file.

The third is judgment. This is where experienced people create real value: interpreting policy, evaluating materiality, understanding context, resolving ambiguity and taking accountability for consequential decisions.

The objective of AI should not be to eliminate the third bucket. It should be to move the first bucket away from people and make the second bucket smaller and more precise.

This creates a different way to evaluate mortgage technology.

For each workflow, lenders could establish a baseline for the human minutes required per completed loan and then break those minutes into reasons: document handling, data reconciliation, routine validation, exception resolution, judgment and rework.

A useful implementation should reduce human-attention minutes without increasing defects, rework or downstream risk. It should also increase the percentage of routine checks completed without human intervention and reduce the number of low-value exceptions sent to reviewers.

This is more revealing than an accuracy number alone. A system can report 98% accuracy and still create limited operating leverage if reviewers must check every output. Conversely, a narrowly scoped system that reliably completes a defined set of checks without human involvement may produce a much larger economic benefit.

This metric also changes capacity planning. Instead of asking how many loans one employee can process, an operations leader can ask how much expert attention a given volume will require and where that attention is likely to be needed.

That becomes particularly important as volumes change. Technology should allow capacity to flex without forcing expertise to scale linearly with every additional loan.

The mortgage industry does not need another race to remove people from the process. It needs a better way to use them.

Experienced processors, underwriters and QC professionals should not spend their time moving information between systems, repeating checks or searching through hundreds of pages to find the evidence behind an exception.

Their attention is expensive because it is valuable.

As AI becomes more capable, lenders should judge it by a simple operational test: After the technology has done its work, how much human attention is still required to complete the loan correctly?

If that number does not materially change, neither will the economics.

New Home Star’s proprietary target consumer profile data tracks purchase priorities across age groups, geographies, home types and price points. Over the past two years, one priority has moved decisively to the top: affordability.

Affordability has become the leading purchase priority across New Home Star’s target consumer profile data, including among buyers who historically may have been less sensitive to it. “Even buyers that are purchasing million-dollar homes, when we ask them to sort their priorities for us, affordability used to be much lower, not even in their top five,” said Dan White, Lead Market Strategist at New Home Star. “We’re seeing even in those buyers, their top response right now is affordability.” But homebuyer affordability looks different depending on the buyer. #12. America’s Landlords Are Aging. Will Their Heirs Take On the Job?

America’s landlords are getting older, setting up a generational handoff that could reshape how millions of rentals are owned and operated.

About 11.2 million U.S. rental properties are owned by individual investors. Among them, roughly 6.9 million—62%—are managed by the owner or an unpaid family member, according to U.S. Census Bureau data.

And many of the people behind those family-run rentals are reaching retirement age and beyond. Nearly 40% of U.S. households receiving rental income are headed by someone 65 or older, compared with 30.6% of households overall.

“The sandwich generation doesn’t want to be the one who gets the call when a toilet breaks at 3 a.m.,” says Scott Stollar, a San Diego real estate agent and certified probate and trust specialist who says he has consulted about 30 families on inherited or handed-down homes over the past two years.

That puts rental property squarely in the Great Wealth Transfer, as $124 trillion is projected to pass from baby boomers and older generations through 2048.

But the transfer is arriving across a stark generational divide in housing. Younger adults are nearly three times as likely to rent, and they are more supportive of limits on landlord power: In a late-2025 poll, 67% of adults aged 18 to 29 favored rent control, compared with 52% of those 65 and older.

That could make the coming transfer look like a generational clash over landlording itself. But the brokers, property managers, and estate attorneys handling these transitions describe a much more practical divide.

Heirs want the wealth associated, they say, but what they are less eager to inherit is the labor, risk, and hands-on business that built it.

“Most heirs do not want to inherit real estate once they realize most real estate is not really ‘passive income’ as it is often portrayed,” says Todd J. Drowlette, managing director of TITAN Commercial Realty Group.

Part of the problem is that the economics of owning rental property have drastically changed.

By 2023, operating expenses on one-unit rentals had climbed to about $8,670 per unit, up roughly 40% from $6,194 in 2020, according to the Rental Housing Finance Survey. National asking rents, meanwhile, peaked that year and have since edged lower, according to data from Realtor.com®.

It’s set up a tough environment of rising costs and diminishing returns, especially when paired with the growing popularity of rent control policies that restrict how much revenue landlords can bring in to offset rising operating expenses.

New York City offers an extreme example. The city’s own data shows operating costs for stabilized buildings rose 5.3% in the latest year, including a 10.5% jump in insurance after an 18.7% increase the year before.

For a family-owned business, it's an especially punishing squeeze.

Ann Korchak’s family has owned rental property in New York since 1941. She now manages two 10-unit buildings on the Upper West Side and has family members living alongside tenants.

In previous reporting for Realtor.com, Korchak estimated that 75% to 80% of her rent roll goes toward property taxes, insurance, utilities, and water and sewer costs. She said the property tax bill on each building had climbed from roughly $33,000 two decades ago to nearly $100,000.

The age of the housing itself adds another complication. The median U.S. rental home is now 45 years old, and more than 42% of renter-occupied single-family homes have some repair need.

“The older generation tended to want to do everything themselves,” says Jessa Gary, an estate planning attorney and partner at Isaac Wiles. “The younger generation saw what Mom and Dad went through, and they don’t necessarily want that to be their reality.”

For heirs who want to preserve the asset without taking on the hands-on job, there is an obvious alternative: Pay someone else to run it.

“What surprised me is how often they keep the home as a rental, even when it was their loved one’s primary residence,” Stollar says of the families he has worked with. “Most of them hire a property manager because they don’t live in the area.”

Sylvie McCracken, an Oregon property manager, says about 15% of her portfolio now involves some form of generational handoff.

But while she often sees heirs keep the real estate, their approach is markedly different from their parents'.

“Their parents ran it like a relationship. Heirs run it like an investment,” McCracken says. “They want reporting, a clear return, written leases, and a plan to bring the property up to standard and closer to market rent.”

That difference could represent a significant change for renters.

A parent may have treated the property partly as a long-term relationship with tenants. An heir may evaluate the same home through return, expenses, and market rent.

There is some evidence that ownership structure already affects those decisions.

In a 2022 Urban Institute survey of landlords, 52% of landlords with one property said they planned to raise rent, compared with 92% of landlords with more than 10 units. One-property landlords planning increases were also more likely to keep them below 5%.

Of course, that doesn't mean an inherited mom-and-pop rental will suddenly behave like one owned by a larger operator. But the Urban Institute data points to what tenants could lose if more rentals move out of the hands of very small landlords: Owners with larger portfolios were much more likely to raise rents and by larger amounts.

Other heirs reach a different conclusion: The property may still hold substantial wealth, but keeping it as a rental no longer makes sense.

Tim Moldenhauer, a real estate broker in Arkansas, recently worked with three siblings who inherited a townhome their parents had rented out to college students.

The property produced about $1,200 a month in profit for their parents. Split among three children, that same income amounted to roughly $400 a month each.

“When the parents died, the kids that inherited the property didn't see the value in continuing to rent it out because between the three kids, the profit would only be about $400 per month,” Moldenhauer says. “They opted to sell the property, and we helped them sell it.”

And this is where the larger market impact may emerge. Multiply that decision across thousands of aging small landlords, and the handoff could start to change which homes remain rentals.

Recent evidence suggests smaller landlords are more likely than large investors to return homes to the owner-occupied market.

A 2026 Cleveland Fed analysis of seven large counties in Ohio and Pennsylvania found that single-family homes owned by smaller investors were more likely to become owner-occupied within three years than those held by larger investors.

But selling doesn't always mean a home leaves the rental market. Another small landlord can buy it, or it can become part of a larger portfolio.

The latter has become more common over time. Between 2001 and 2021, the share of single-family rentals owned by nonindividual investors rose from 17% to 25%, according to Harvard’s analysis of U.S. Census data. Among two- to four-unit rental properties, the share more than doubled, from 15% to 32%.

For heirs, selling can also change what gets passed down, according to Barry E. Janay, an estate planning attorney and founder of the Law Office of Barry E. Janay.

“Many times, the parents will direct their assets to be liquidated and just give the kids cash to go on with their lives,” he says.

Still, other families preserve the real estate but make it easier for the next generation to manage.

Stollar, the San Diego real estate agent, recently worked with a family that sold several properties in Santa Barbara and used a 1031 exchange to buy properties closer to their daughter in San Diego, where she could manage them.

“Some families don’t wait to hand it off," he says.

#15. Ready for surging short sales in 2027?

Mortgage servicers are offering more pre-foreclosure short sale options to a rapidly growing pool of underwater homeowners who are also behind on mortgage payments

A rapidly rising number of underwater homeowners who are also behind on mortgage payments means short sales will likely be rising in the next 12 months. Mortgage servicers are already starting to proactively prepare for the coming surge.

There were 320,000 homes nationwide with both negative equity — where the estimated value of the home is worth less than the outstanding mortgage balance — and where the borrower was behind on mortgage payments as of August 2026, according to data from Intercontinental Exchange (ICE). That was more than double the 163,000 from a year earlier.

Additionally, ICE reported that 813,000 total homes were underwater — had negative equity — as of August, up by 250,000 (44%) from a year ago.

An analysis of public record data from Cotality shows the number of negative equity homeowners increasing by more than half a million between July 2025 and September 2026, a 20% increase.

The same data shows more dramatic increases for certain loan types: Underwater mortgages insured by the Veterans Administration (VA) rose 44%, while underwater mortgages insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) rose 38%. The number of underwater mortgages was up 7% for conventional mortgages, which includes those backed by the government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

These numbers point to increasing short sales in 2027 as the two-trigger combination of delinquency and negative equity pushes more homeowners to sell short to avoid foreclosure.

Short sales have already begun to trend higher. Short sales had increased for four consecutive quarters to a five-year high of 8,158 in the second quarter of 2026, according to public record data from Cotality. The short sale numbers are still well below what they were in the wake of the Great Financial Crisis. Between 2009 and 2012, there were an average of about 72,000 short sales a quarter, according to the Cotality data.

Although still at a very low level, short sales increased 76% in the second quarter of 2026 compared to a year earlier for properties with loans backed by the GSEs. There were 283 short sales on GSE-backed loans in Q2 2026, up from 155 a year earlier, according to the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA).

Pre-foreclosure sales on loans insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) increased 33% in 2025 to a four-year high of 1,510, according to FHA’s annual report to Congress.

Government agencies like FHA, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, along with mortgage servicers, are taking note of this emerging upward trend in short sales and pre-foreclosure sales — along with the upward trend in delinquent mortgages that are underwater. They are starting to add options to their loss mitigation waterfall that will help accommodate the increasing volume.

One such option is SmartSale, which allows individual homeowners to list their property for sale on Auction.com as well as the Multiple Listing Service (MLS). Auction.com launched SmartSale last year with distressed homeowners facing foreclosure in mind. A growing number of mortgage servicers are informing distressed homeowners about options like SmartSale, particularly those homeowners who have run out of any loss mitigation options that would allow them to stay in the home.

The number of individual homeowners interested in listing their property on Auction.com through SmartSale increased by 91% in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter. That number increased by 76% for homeowners coming directly to Auction.com and by an eye-popping 157% for homeowners who learned about the SmartSale option through their mortgage servicer.

“When we met, and we were sitting outside of her house, she said, ‘Please help me,’” recalled the broker, Tavia Dowden. “When she said this, I was like, I have to get this house sold for her.”

The pre-foreclosure short sale also allowed the homeowner to transition gracefully out of a home she could no longer afford and into new housing that was more suitable for her budget and lifestyle.

“There’s brand new…senior apartments here in the town,” she said in a phone call with Auction.com. “There are only 38 units, and I managed to snag one. I’m moving in tomorrow, so I’m very excited.”

#20. Stanley Martin Homes’ Strategy for Building a Southeast Powerhouse

The Nathaniel floor plan at Stanley Martin Homes' Southbridge master-planned community in Sumter, South Carolina.

Over the course of Stanley Martin Homes’ 60 years in business, countless strategic decisions have helped the Reston, Virginia-based company grow from a single-market operator to a regional powerhouse that ranks No. 18 on the Builder 100 list.

However, for CEO Steve Alloy one of the most significant moments for Stanley Martin was a philosophic shift that occurred eight years after he assumed the CEO role. With this shift in the mid-2000s, Stanley Martin Homes went from being a project-oriented company to a people- and culture-oriented one.

“We shifted away from [project-oriented thinking] and said ‘We’re building an organization for the long term, and the projects will come and go,’” Alloy tells BUILDER. “That really changed everything, because we attracted a different type of person to join us.”

This people-focused approach has shaped the strategic decisions Stanley Martin Homes has made in the ensuing years, from its first expansion of operations to Charlottesville and Richmond, Virginia, and Raleigh in 2013 and 2014 to partnering with Daiwa House in 2017 to the six builder acquisitions the company has made in the past decade.

The people-focused approach is particularly noticeable in Stanley Martin’s approach to M&A.

With most of its acquisitions, the company’s intention is to retain the team and allow their local market expertise to guide Stanley Martin’s approach in each new market.

“Our approach has been to bring in local expertise for relationships, for long local history, all of those things, and then combine it with our operating platform,” Alloy says. “Where we’ve got capital access, low cost of capital, great technology, strong processes, strong quality control and blend that with the local deep market knowledge.”

This approach allows for many synergies to occur, according to Alloy. Cost structures, finance costs, and insurance costs go down while rebates increase. However, beyond the synergies, Stanley Martin’s goal is always to provide the local teams with more resources to succeed than they could have had operating independently.

“You get local talent and it has a platform that allows them to do more than they were doing before,” Alloy notes.

The strategy does have exceptions, though. The acquisition of United Homes Group was unique for Stanley Martin in a number of ways. Firstly, United Homes was a public company with many overlapping markets with Stanley Martin. While recent deals to acquire Windsor Homes and Holiday Builders gave Staney Martin access to almost exclusively new markets in North Carolina and Florida, the United Homes Group acquisition provided entrance to the Myrtle Beach market while also enhancing existing divisions across South Carolina and Georgia.

Additionally, with United Homes Group, many of the corporate leadership team had the intention to depart after the sale of the company rather than stay on following the merger. Alloy says the broader model has remained consistent through its acquisitions of Windsor Homes, Avex Homes, Essex Homes, FrontDoor Communities, and Holiday Builders.

Stanley Martin’s partnership with Japanese company Daiwa House has provided the builder with low-cost capital, a long-term focus, and a goal to expand rapidly.

When the companies joined forces in 2017, Stanley Martin was coming off a year in which it closed just over 800 homes. When the company reports its annual numbers for 2026, Alloy says its closings will be 10 times greater.

Daiwa House’s approach to its U.S. subsidiaries is more hands-off than other Japanese companies with stakes in the U.S. home building sector.

“Daiwa House doesn’t drive Stanley Martin’s strategy, Daiwa House defers to their U.S. team,” Alloy explains. “Our CEO believes that there are major cultural differences and business practice differences between Japanese businesses and U.S. businesses. His belief is to find the right U.S. leaders and enable them to make the key decisions in the U.S.”

As a result, Stanley Martin’s relationship with its parent company is an extension of how it approaches its own acquisitions: pair local leadership and expertise with the capabilities and resources of a much larger organization.

The relationship has helped transform Stanley Martin and helped open Alloy’s eyes in terms of what is possible for the regional builder.

“In terms of scale, I never imagined we could be a top five or a top three home builder back in the day,” he says. “But that’s what we intend to do. The sights are set higher because we’re part of something that’s just so much bigger. It really never occurred to us that it was possible before.”

While unit closings are not the lone metric for success at Stanley Martin Homes, the past 13 months represent the builder’s aspirations to become a top-five builder. Between the acquisitions of United Homes Group, Holiday Builders, and Windsor Homes, the company added approximately 2,400 annual closings and fully fleshed out its southeastern portfolio.

“The thing that ties them together is each of those companies were in strategic markets for us that were adjacent to where we were,” Alloy explains.

The tagline of Stanley Martin’s strategic plan for 2024 to 2026 was to become a dominant Southeast builder. The builder’s “concentration strategy” in this period was focused on building up a bigger presence in the Southeast rather than expanding nationally.

In the Maryland-to-Georgia territory, the lone two major housing markets Stanley Martin was not in were Greensboro, North Carolina, and Myrtle Beach. With the acquisitions of Windsor Homes and United Homes Group, respectively, helped fill out the portfolio.

The acquisition of Holiday Builders helped address Stanley Martin’s limited presence in Florida.

“The big missing piece was that we were too small in Florida. In 2025, we were the 25th biggest builder in the state. We were in Orlando, with a little bit in Tampa,” Alloy says. “Next year, we’ll be No. 6 in the state because we pursued a company in Florida that added all the adjacent markets that we weren’t in.”

After addressing market holes through expansion, Stanley Martin’s strategic plan for 2027 to 2029 is focused on scaling within its existing markets. The builder’s concentration strategy calls for Stanley Martin Homes to have a top-three market share in each of its 23 markets.

Currently, Alloy says, Stanley Martin has a top-three market share in eight markets and a top-five market share in 12 markets. According to Zonda Local Leaders data, Stanley Martin Homes is the second-largest builder in the Washington, D.C-Arlington, Virginia-Alexandria, Virginia; Richmond, Virginia; and Columbia, South Carolina, markets.

“Our strategy for the next three years is more reliant on concentration than new markets and spreading out,” he says. “We have 15 markets that we have to move from where we are to the top three.”

Maintaining a unified culture across 23 operating divisions is more difficult than when Stanley Martin operated in a single market. Each division inevitably takes on some characteristics of the leadership team in place at the division level, Alloy notes.

“There’s an overlay of how we approach culture and we’re very disciplined around our approach to people as well as our approach to technology,” he says.

Instead of a traditional human resources team, Stanley Martin has a team and culture department. The department’s function is to ensure cultural alignment and prioritize investment, systems, and processes that can be implemented to ensure it is maintained.

“We are continuing to focus on how we keep the culture that has made us successful, and we help the people side by giving them the best and easiest tools to succeed,” Alloy says.

Internal projections suggest Stanley Martin should be a top 10 builder by 2028 as a standalone company. Yet he says size itself will not be the ultimate measure of success moving forward. Instead, the same ingredients that helped turn a single-market builder into the regional player Stanley Martin is today will remain at the center of the strategy.

“The key ingredients are getting the people right, getting the technology right, enabling people to make decisions, and drive the business,” Alloy says. “In the end, if we have the right outcomes for our people and we have the right outcomes for our customers, everything worked.”

The first step to writing compelling Facebook ad copy is gaining a deep understanding of your target audience. By knowing their demographics, preferences, and pain points, you can tailor your copy to resonate with their needs and desires. Conduct thorough market research, utilize Facebook's audience insights, and analyze data from previous campaigns to gather valuable information about your target audience.

The headline is the first element of your Facebook ad copy that users will see. It needs to be attention-grabbing and compelling enough to entice people to read further. Use action-oriented words, emphasize unique selling propositions, and create a sense of urgency to pique the audience's curiosity and encourage them to click on your ad.

When writing Facebook ad copy, it's crucial to highlight the benefits of the property or service you're promoting, rather than merely listing its features. Benefits explain how the property or service can improve the lives of potential buyers or renters. By emphasizing the value and advantages, you can create an emotional connection with your audience and motivate them to take action.

Storytelling is a powerful tool for capturing the attention of your audience and making your Facebook ad copy more relatable. Craft a compelling narrative that showcases the unique aspects of the property or the positive experiences of previous clients. Engaging stories can evoke emotions, create a sense of authenticity, and build trust with potential clients.

Social proof, such as testimonials, reviews, or success stories, can significantly influence potential clients' decision-making process. Incorporate social proof into your Facebook ad copy to build credibility and trust. Highlight positive feedback from satisfied clients or showcase awards and recognition you've received in the real estate industry.

Continuous testing and optimization are essential for improving the performance of your Facebook ad copy. Create multiple variations of your ad copy, test them against each other, and analyze the results. Fine-tune your copy based on metrics such as click-through rates (CTR), conversion rates, and engagement levels to identify what resonates best with your audience.

Writing compelling Facebook ad copy is a crucial skill for real estate agents looking to excel in their digital marketing efforts. By understanding their target audience, crafting attention-grabbing headlines, focusing on benefits, incorporating storytelling techniques, and utilizing social proof, agents can create persuasive and engaging copy that captivates potential clients. By mastering the art of writing Facebook ad copy, real estate agents can unlock the full potential of social media advertising and boost their success in the competitive real estate market.

#25. The Biggest Credit Hurdle for Renters Who Want To Buy

What’s the biggest payment you make every month? If you’re a renter, it’s probably your rent.

But did you know that you can pay your rent on time, every time, for years, and it may never show up in your credit history? It’s pretty frustrating, especially if you’d like to buy a home someday and you’re trying to build your credit.

The good thing is that’s changing right now. Here’s what you need to know and why it could open a door you thought was closed.

You’re not the only one frustrated. According to Zillow, nearly a third of applicants (32.88%) get denied a mortgage due to insufficient credit history – not missed payments or too much debt. Just not enough credit history for a lender to say “yes.”

It’s the most common hurdle would-be buyers have. As Zillow explains:

“Nearly one-third of mortgage applicants are denied a loan because of insufficient credit history, making it the most common reason for a denial.”

But there’s finally a way in now that how credit gets measured is changing.

As Michael Sherman, General Manager and Senior VP at Zillow Rentals, explains:

“Affordability is the headline in housing right now, and access to financing is a very important part of the story. Credit history is a barrier standing in the way of too many potential buyers. Rent reporting is a direct way to address that.”

Here’s what Sherman is talking about. New credit models are emerging that take on-time rent and some utility payments into consideration to help renters get credit for the bills they already pay.

VantageScore is one company with a new credit model that has been approved for use by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). And that roll out could have a big impact on renters like you.

A recent study by VantageScore found that nearly 4 million U.S. renters could finally become eligible for a mortgage if their on-time rent payments were counted as part of their credit history. Now, this isn’t a guarantee that it will suddenly count for every would-be buyer, but it does crack open the door.

Here’s the catch though, you still have to walk through it.

There are more options available to build credit, but you have to be proactive. Your rent won’t necessarily count unless you make sure it does. And the sooner you take credit for your payments, the better.

Here’s how to get started based on a few tips from Experian, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), and FICO:

Ask your landlord or property manager if your rent is being reported. If it’s not, opt in.

If your landlord doesn’t offer rent reporting, there are services available. Be sure to check fees and which bureaus they provide your data to.

Check your credit reports for errors. Mistakes happen and fixing them is one of the quickest ways to strengthen your profile. The CFPB offers step-by-step instructions on how to fix errors on your report.

Follow basic, important credit rules. Pay your bills on time, keep your card balances low, and don’t apply for a bunch of new credit at once.

Don’t close any accounts – even if you have paid them off and don’t use them often.

Diversify your profile by getting a secured credit card or becoming an authorized user on someone else’s account to help you build history beyond rent.

These steps take a little time and effort, but it could be worth it if it starts building up your credit history.

Just remember, not every lender uses the same scoring model and there are more changes on the horizon. So, the sooner you get credit for the work you do, the better.

Even if you’re not sure when you might be ready to buy a home, talking to a lender is a good place to start. A professional loan officer will help you make a plan and help you figure out a timeline to get ready to officially apply and buy.

#27. First American’s Odeta Kushi on how labor, AI and migration will impact the housing market

First American economist Odeta Kushi explains how mortgage rates above 7% and annual home price growth of 1% affect affordability. She also discusses labor market stickiness, AI productivity and shifting migration patterns.  AI Summary

Odeta Kushi, deputy chief economist at First American, says that the 2027 housing market is likely to be constrained due to higher mortgage rates, but factors like household income growth and cooler home price appreciation will present opportunities for housing professionals and their clients.

Kushi, who’s been with First American for more than a decade, sat down with HousingWire at last week’s Mortgage Bankers Association Annual Conference & Expo in Chicago. She discussed a range of topics tied to housing affordability and supply, the labor market, artificial intelligence and aging in place.

Editor’s note: This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Neil Pierson: Let’s start by talking about housing affordability issues. First American’s most recent Real House Price Index showed that the U.S. is about 5% less affordable than the same point last year. In the face of higher mortgage rates and other factors, what do you expect to happen in the months ahead?

Odeta Kushi: I would expect some further deterioration now that rates have climbed above 7% and have stayed there. I would expect the mortgage rate piece to drag affordability lower, but I would say the upside is we’re still seeing income growth outpace house price growth. That’s been the case all year. That’s in part because we continue to see positive income growth, but also because house price growth has been quite slow.

Our index shows that we’re at 1% year-over-year house price growth. Historically, we’re closer to 3.5%. It’s pretty slow at a national level, and that’s being dragged lower by a lot of the Sun Belt markets that we know are now seeing depreciation. But then you have places like Chicago where prices are still showing 5% to 6% growth year over year, so some of those regional variations and that push-pull dynamic has contributed to pretty slow growth.

Pierson: What are your views on the current labor market? Some people view it as stagnant, but if that were really true, wouldn’t the Federal Reserve be doing more to spur activity? Their officials don’t seem overly concerned.

Kushi: I would argue that a resilient labor market has allowed the Fed to increase interest rates because they feel comfortable trying to slow down the economy without tipping it into a job-loss recession. I would classify it as stable as of right now.

And not to overuse the term “low hire, low fire,” but I do think that there’s something to that. We’re in a labor market where employers aren’t necessarily hiring, and they’re also not firing, which means employees are staying put. They’re not switching jobs very often, which is a big trigger for buying and selling a home.

I think that the labor market sort of being stuck has direct implications for the housing market and the fact that the housing market has been sort of frozen as well. Once we start to see the labor market pick up, I think you’ll start to see some of those positive effects on the housing market as well.

For five consecutive months as of August, the rate of inflation has been above wage growth, which is a bit concerning for the health of the consumer. But that positive wage growth has been part of the reason that the economy has been so resilient for so long.

Pierson: Fed officials have spoken about on the impact of artificial intelligence on the economy. How do you view that trend? Is it going to be a deteriorating factor going forward if AI essentially replaces some jobs?

Kushi: That remains to be seen. There’s obviously been some pressure for younger workers entering the workforce, but it’s difficult to disentangle whether that’s really an AI impact. I’m sure some of it is, but it’s also tied to the point in the labor market that we’ve been in, which is cyclical cooling.

Historically, we do see that impact to younger people, college grads, entering the workforce. But in fact, you could see a sort of Jevons paradox — essentially where this AI revolution actually makes people more productive and more efficient. The example I’ve given in the past is that maybe a law clerk or a lawyer has traditionally been cost prohibitive, so not a lot of people use them. But suddenly that lawyer becomes more productive. They have these AI research assistants. They can do more with their resources and so it actually becomes cheaper to use them and you actually demand more lawyers.

These AI efficiencies could actually cause demand for labor to go up in certain sectors. And I think there’s some anecdotes of that in the mortgage industry. But AI productivity enhancements take a long time to show up in the data. There’s a famous quote: “You can see the computer age everywhere, except in productivity statistics.” So it could be very similar for AI as well.

Pierson: Let’s talk about migration as there seems to be stagnancy around that too. People are moving less often. But are there any hot spots in the country you’re watching, where people are attracted by the job market and relative affordability, but migration could drive up home prices in the long run?

Kushi: What we’ve seen in the last couple of years is the Sun Belt markets have really been the domestic in-migration hubs. A lot of people moved into Florida and Texas during the pandemic. That’s still the case. Those Sun Belt markets are still popular.

When we look at the population growth stats, North Carolina and South Carolina are up there. Texas is still positive, but the rate of growth is slower than it was at the peak pandemic rate.

The area I’m watching closely is the Midwest. We’ve seen positive domestic in-migration there. I was in Ohio recently. That’s certainly a state where we’ve seen people move in — and that hasn’t been the case in a long time. Mostly, we’ve seen people move out. And I think that’s a relative affordability story. There are very few parts of the country where you can find a home priced below $300,000 these days, and I think that’s part of the story happening in the Midwest.

Pierson: There’s a lack of inventory for senior homeowners, which is a key trend for reverse mortgage professionals. Many seniors might have looked to downsize in the past, but today they can’t find an affordable price point to let go of their low mortgage rate and open up supply for the first-time buyer. Is that problem addressed specifically through increased supply, or do you need a way to incentivize these people to move?

Kushi: We do see the aging-in-place trend that’s been happening for some time. Technology has enabled seniors to live in their homes comfortably and for their homes to meet their needs. We do generally expect what we’re calling the “silver housing handoff” to occur gradually over the next decade or so, and that’s just simply due to aging out of their homes.

We know historically that when people hit 80 and above, they tend to age out of their homes into retirement communities, or they die, of course. We do expect that trend to unleash homes on the market. This is a huge demographic. They own a lot of homes in prime locations, but those homes will need some work, some renovation, to be made attractive to the next generation of buyers.

That will also happen over time and that could speed the transition to the younger, potentially first-time homebuyers. But these homes tend to be bigger; they’re not necessarily starter homes. They’re not one to two bedrooms. They’re three to five bedrooms. So they may skip that potential first-time buyer and go to the people looking for their forever home.

Pierson: HUD officials Joe Gormley and Matt Jones spoke at MBA Annual about renovation financing, which could be beneficial in these situations.

#46. 6 Tips for a Successful Customer-First Website

The days of home builder websites functioning as simple lead-generation tool are over. Today’s home buyers expect something much different than a landing page with a phone number or model home address. They want details and plenty of visuals to form an opinion.

Embrace radical transparency. All five professionals agreed that buyers want to know the details down to price and payment. Duderstadt asks, “How can I be more transparent in everything about the home that I’m selling. We need to tell our consumers more. They’re craving more.”

McCarty agrees that buyers need access to pricing, options, specifications, and ownership-cost information before they are ready to engage with a sales team. She says, “A consumer can’t make an educated decision on their future without all of that.”

“We want customers to understand exactly what they’re buying and what it costs before they sign a contract,” Nelson echoes. Transparency is no longer a competitive advantage, but an expectation.

Design around the buyer’s journey. The most impactful websites are built around how consumers shop rather than around internal product structures, company terminology, or community hierarchies.

Hannah Worth and Ally Price believe every design decision should start with a simple principle: “Design around the buyer’s questions.”

Navigation, filters, searches, and content organization should help buyers quickly answer basic questions: Where do you build? What homes are available? What do they cost? Which options fit my needs?

McCarty also stresses the importance of flexibility saying that “No two customers are going to go through the journey the same way.”

Similarly, Duderstadt notes that different buyers care about different details. One customer may be focused on construction quality and insulation performance, while another is evaluating curb appeal, finishes, or interior design options. The challenge for marketers is delivering the right information to the right shopper at the right time.

Remove friction everywhere. Because home builders’ websites serve as a convenience factor, the experience should be fast and barrier-free. McCarty says, “The digital experience is about low friction and low effort.”

Reducing complexity and helping buyers accomplish tasks quickly make for a more successful site. Price agrees that the site should “make the next step easy.”

Fast load times, intuitive navigation, accessible design, and straightforward forms all contribute to a smoother experience. Every additional click, tricky menu, or unnecessary form field creates an opportunity for a potential buyer to leave.

Invest in visuals. As industry pros continue evaluating their sites, they are finding that visuals are one of the most important website assets. Nelson says, “Video consistently outperforms static imagery in terms of clicks and leads.”

His team has expanded its use of videos, drone footage, renderings, photography, and home visualizers to help shoppers better understand both quick move-in and to-be-built homes.

Duderstadt points to similar tools including interactive floor plans. He adds, “You need to have rendered versions of products that you haven’t built yet.”

Combining inspiration and practicality, Fischer’s Worth and Price say that compelling photography, floor plans, and local information can create a stronger website with the objective being to move buyers seamlessly from “I love this” to “Could this work for me?”

Personalize the experience. With added visuals and content, the next challenge for home builder websites is helping buyers find what is most important to them.

For M/I’s Duderstadt, personalization represents one of the industry’s biggest opportunities. “Figuring out how you deliver the right content to the right person as content overall increases, that’s where magic starts to happen for a marketer,” he says.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to play a heavy role in that effort as both Nelson and McCarty mention it can help buyers identify homes and communities more quickly while also collecting meaningful data.

Price sees similar potential, noting that AI can help shoppers narrow choices and find relevant answers faster when supported by strong content and accurate data.

Test, measure, and optimize. Ringing clearly throughout every conversation was the simple lesson that great websites are never finished. Nelson’s advice is straightforward. He says, “Test everything.”

In addition to A/B testing, visitor behavior analysis, conversion tracking, and ongoing optimization to improve performance, builders need to revisit and revisit often to fit not only today’s buyer but the future’s.

Both Worth and Price say that marketers should focus on understanding what visitors accomplish and where they encounter obstacles. Price adds, “We also review performance by source, device, market, and page type to identify opportunities for improvement.

“Context matters: a longer session can reflect strong interest, but it can also indicate that a visitor is having difficulty finding information. The goal is to understand what visitors accomplish and where they encounter friction.”

The formula for a successful home building site is consistent across each expert’s advice. Add more info, more transparency, high-quality visuals, and remove friction. As McCarty puts it, successful websites should focus on removing barriers buyers do not care about to help them make confident decisions about their purchase, which is the top mark of success.

#57. Is ‘Giving While Living’ the Best Way To Hand Down Your Home to the Next Generation?

Whether you call it giving while living, giving with warm hands rather than cold, or a living inheritance, the prospect of sharing your wealth with the next generation before you pass away is becoming more mainstream.

In an interview with Fortune, Penny Pennington, managing partner of wealth management firm Edward Jones, described how clients are embracing the practice, and the publication reported that a “growing number” of the firm’s 9 million clients are doing so.

A study from Wealth.com found that financial advisers say 46% of clients who plan to transfer assets also intend to give some of their wealth during their lifetimes.

Giving while living has mostly been a conversation among wealthy families. For everyone else, the question gets harder, because the home is often the most valuable thing a family owns.

In general, what you give matters as much as whether you give. If the goal is to give your children a home, handing over the house can work. If the goal is to pass on as much value as possible, the house itself may not be the right gift.

Sharing your wealth with the next generation, or generations, can take a number of forms, including gifting money for a home down payment, college tuition, or other major expense.

Handing down your home directly to the next generation, while you’re still alive, is perhaps the most consequential gift you could give your children. The economics and considerations of such a gift have changed in recent years.

“I’m seeing a real shift in how families talk about that wealth. The old assumption was that children would inherit the assets when their parents passed. Now more parents are asking: Why wait? Why not gift it while their children are younger and can use it to build their own wealth through real estate?” says Cynthia Mattiza, a luxury real estate broker with Sotheby's in West Austin and the Hill Country in Texas.  “A down payment at 32 changes a life far more than an inheritance at 58.”

This is especially true given the current housing market, which is marked by a lack of affordable housing and elevated interest rates, which keep many younger families on the homebuying sidelines. By handing down the home, older homeowners help their children bypass some of the biggest sticking points of homebuying today, such as carrying a mortgage, paying private mortgage insurance, and finding a home in the first place.

The most obvious benefit is in the name: Parents get to watch the gift land, and it can arrive when a child needs it most.

"You get to see the recipient enjoy the benefits of your generosity," says Rick Simonetti, head of wealth planning at Fidelis Capital Partners. "Waiting until you pass, when the recipients are in their 50s, 60s, or 70s, creates quite a different outcome than giving during life."

Mattiza says even part of the proceeds from a sale can change the math.

"I closed a transaction last month where the buyer paid cash using funds from their parents rather than take on a mortgage at 7.1%," she says. "That family turned a gift into immediate equity, with no monthly interest working against them."

There can be financial upsides for the giver, too. Transferring a home out of an estate reduces the taxable estate value upon death. However, with the individual estate tax exemption at $15 million in 2026, this reduction primarily affects high net worth estates.

And giving the home away more than five years before applying for Medicaid long-term care coverage generally avoids a penalty, since the program reviews transfers made within that window.

A home is the asset where giving early can cost the most, because of how the tax code treats what children inherit versus what they are given.

"Gifting the home itself is usually the worst way to give," says Mattiza. "Your kids take your original cost basis and lose the step-up."

She offers an example: "Parents bought in Westlake, TX, in 1995 for $400,000 and the home is now worth $2.5 million. Gift it and the kids sell: roughly $2.1 million in taxable gain. Inherit it: The basis resets to $2.5 million and the gain essentially disappears. The house also loses the parents' homestead exemption and over-65 tax ceiling, so property taxes can jump."

Gifting a home can also trigger other tax consequences, including a federal gift tax return and the loss of local property tax breaks, and the rules vary by state, so a CPA should review the numbers first.

There is also a question that has to be answered before any gift: What happens to the parents next.

"Retirement can last 30 years or longer, and nursing home care can cost well over $100,000 annually in many areas," says Pamela Garrett, an attorney and the owner of Law Mother Asset Protection & Estate Planning. "Before giving away significant assets, parents need to consider future medical expenses, assisted living, long-term care, inflation, and the possibility of outliving their savings."

On airplanes, passengers are told to put on their own oxygen mask before helping others. The same rule applies here: Secure your own future first, because there may be little recourse afterward.

"One of the greatest gifts parents can give their children is ensuring they have sufficient resources to care for themselves throughout retirement, including unexpected medical and long-term care expenses," Garrett says. "Once assets are gifted outright, parents generally cannot demand them back if their financial circumstances change."

Cash is often a better candidate for lifetime giving, Garrett says, because it doesn't carry an embedded capital gain. Parents can also move wealth through annual gift tax exclusions (up to $19,000 per recipient per year, as of 2026), or by paying certain education and medical expenses directly.

For parents who want to plan for the home without handing it over now, a revocable living trust is an option. It is not a lifetime gift, and that's the point.

"A properly funded revocable living trust can allow a home to pass outside probate without requiring parents to surrender control during their lifetime," Garrett says. "Because the home generally remains included in the parents' estate, it also generally preserves the opportunity for a step-up in basis at death."

The trade-off is timing. Parents keep control of the house and the step-up, but their children don't get the home while they are younger. Parents who still want to give the house during their lives should talk with a CPA, an estate attorney, and a trusted real estate agent first, since the right structure depends on state rules and on how the family plans to use the home.

A useful way to start is at the end, working backward from what the family hopes to achieve. That means talking early.

"Start with a conversation about wealth strategy, not a specific house. What are the family's goals? Is this about helping one child now, or a plan for everyone over time?" says Mattiza. "Have the conversation before anyone starts house hunting. Not in the middle of a bidding war."

#60. Myth vs. Reality: Does the Fed Control Mortgage Rates?

You’ve probably heard the Federal Reserve (the Fed) is raising rates again. And if you’re planning to buy or sell a house, you may be wondering what that means for you.

With all the headlines, it’s easy to think the Fed sets mortgage rates. That’s actually a common myth. But the Fed’s decisions can still have an impact on them.

So, the Fed raises its key short-term rate (the Federal Funds Rate) to slow down inflation. And that’s where a lot of people get tripped up.

REALITY: The Fed has an impact on mortgage rates, but it’s only one piece of the puzzle.

“The Federal Reserve influences mortgage rates, but doesn’t set them. . . Mortgage rates are influenced by many elements, including the inflation rate, the pace of job creation, and whether the economy is growing or shrinking. The Federal Reserve’s monetary policy is a factor, too . . .”

Here’s the simplest way to explain how it all fits together. Mortgage rates tend to follow something called the 10-year treasury yield. That’s why any news about the conflict can move mortgage rates. If there’s resolution in Iran, that could take some pressure off inflation and mortgage rates. But the timing is hard to predict.

What the Fed does can move that yield, too. When they hike the Federal Funds Rate to fight inflation, investors pay attention. That can push the 10-year yield up, and mortgage rates usually follow. #69. Hurricane Damage Is Costly—and Your HOA Could Demand Thousands Extra

After a major disaster like a hurricane, an HOA may need to repair common areas and infrastructure, address insurance deductibles and uninsured losses, and restore essential community services.

If insurance proceeds and existing association funds are insufficient, homeowners could be charged a special assessment to fund their share of those costs.

Melanie Zimmerman, owner and real estate agent at MVP Chicago Real Estate in Chicago recently worked with a client who lives in a Chicago area condo and received a whopping special assessment of $7,400.

Part of the building’s roof had sustained wind and water damage. While her personal insurance policy covered the damage inside her unit, it didn’t pay for the building’s insurance deductible.

“She was shocked by the assessment, believing her regular HOA dues would cover expenses like this. Let her story be a reminder that special assessments can result in other surprises and are not covered by your basic HOA dues,” says Zimmerman.

If your home is not in an HOA, however, you carry the full cost of the damage, but have control over every decision involved. You won’t have to navigate disputes over who is responsible for a roof or wall, wait for board member decisions, or pay assessments for community repairs.

Dawn Bauman, CEO of Community Associations Institute in Falls Church, VA, points out that a disaster doesn’t always mean an HOA homeowner will pay more.

“A well-managed association may actually be better prepared because it has been planning collectively for major repairs and emergencies through insurance, reserve funding, maintenance, professional management, and disaster planning,” Bauman explains.

If you live in an HOA, read your governing documents closely. Get up to speed on what’s your responsibility vs. the association’s responsibility, particularly for roofs, windows, doors, landscaping, and other components susceptible to storm damage.

Then, review its insurance coverage and your personal homeowner policy so you know what each policy covers and where gaps may exist.

“Ask your insurance agent specifically about loss-assessment coverage and whether the limits are adequate for your community's potential exposure. Don’t assume your personal coverage will automatically cover every special assessment,” Berger explains.

Check your association's hurricane or named-storm deductible. In a large community, even a percentage deductible can translate into a substantial dollar amount that ultimately must be funded by everyone who lives there.

Also, get involved in your HOA election and vote on all matters.

“Vote in your HOA election each year and support candidates who stress sound fiscal planning and well-funded reserves. That’s what helps communities recover quickly and painlessly from most disasters,” says Berger.

Speaking of reserves, Edward Susolik, president and trial attorney at Callahan & Blaine in Irvine, CA, explains that the time to argue about them is at the annual meeting—not after a natural disaster strikes.

“Once a hurricane happens, the board has the ability to throw on assessments fast. And if you never looked at that budget, you’ve got nothing to leverage,” adds Susolik.

It’s also important to maintain and document your property's pre-storm condition. Preventive measures can reduce damage and may help preserve available insurance rights.

If and when a disaster hits, document damage promptly with date-stamped photographs and video. Then, notify your insurance company and preserve receipts and other records relating to emergency work and repairs.

“Documentation can make the claims process and recovery easier,” says Bauman.

#86. Home Improvement Projects Cost 76% More Than They Did a Decade Ago

The typical home improvement project costs three-quarters more than it did 10 years ago, according to a new report from Lending Tree.

The median cost of a home improvement project rose from $1,200 in 2015 to an inflation-adjusted $2,113 in 2025—a shocking 76% increase.

The largest year-over-year jump happened between 2021 and 2023, when costs rose 22.2%.

Material costs and labor costs have both skyrocketed in recent years, according to Richmond real estate agent Daniel Yoon, causing a steep overall cost increase. Having renovated two homes of his own, Yoon also points out that most projects are bigger than they appear at the start.

“Good tradespeople are booked weeks out, and they price like it. Materials went up after 2020 and never came back down, and tariffs have added to that,” Yoon tells Realtor.com®. “On top of that, a lot of jobs grow once you start. You open a wall in an older house and suddenly you're paying for electrical work and permits you never planned on.”

Homebuilders reported that the cost of key homebuilding materials increased 6.7% over last year, according to the National Association of Home Builders Housing Market Index survey for 2026.

Plus, a worsening shortage of skilled-trades construction workers is costing the United States $10.8 billion per year in added costs, according to the Home Builders Institute.

Still, many homeowners are opting to take on renovations rather than move, often so they can hold onto their lower mortgage rate.

“A lot of people are sitting on a rate around 3%. Selling means giving that up, buying at today's prices and borrowing at today's rates,” says Yoon. “Next to that, even a big kitchen remodel looks like the cheaper option.”

The average cost of routine maintenance and upkeep can change a lot based on when the home was built, according to Lending Tree’s research. Routine maintenance costs roughly $1,433 per year for most homes, but jumps to $1,706 for homes built before 1940.

Homes built after 2015 show steep drops in yearly maintenance costs, with homes built in 2022 and 2023 costing just $173 on average to maintain.

While median listing prices for newly built homes in the United States, at $450,256 in quarter two of 2026, is slightly higher than that of existing homes, which fell to $408,317 in the second quarter, the potential need for repairs on older homes should be taken into consideration.

“Buyers compare list prices, but that's only part of what a house costs you. Richmond has plenty of pre-1940 homes in neighborhoods like the Fan and Church Hill. They're beautiful, but you're often dealing with old plaster, original wiring, cast iron drains, and drafty windows, and that shows up in your budget every year,” says Yoon. “A new build comes with a new roof, new HVAC and a builder warranty, so the first decade is usually pretty quiet.”

He added that builders can often offer rate buydowns and help with closing costs, which individual owners can’t often match.

Lending Tree found that the majority of homeowners are able to fund home improvement projects with cash from savings. The report shows that 75.7% of projects were paid for in cash, far outpacing the next payment options, credit cards at 6.3%, homeowners insurance settlements at 3.9%, and home equity loans at 3%.

However, estimated median cost of repairs also varied by funding source. The median cost of a project paid for in cash was $1,902, whereas the median cost of a rennovation funded by a homeowners insurance settlement was $9,963, and the median funded by a home equity loan was $7,924.

Minor and routine work should be paid in full with cash when possible, but it may be necessary to seek other funding for major repairs, says Matt Schulz, Lending Tree’s chief consumer finance analyst.

“Borrowing against home equity may be reasonable for a large, necessary project with a long useful life, especially when you have strong equity, stable income, and a clear repayment plan,” says Schulz in the report. “However, there’s a risk there. Putting your house up as collateral isn’t something that should be done lightly.”

Homeowners should save money in advance to use on home maintenance—that way, they are prepared for repairs and can avoid taking out loans. Yoon recommends saving 1% to 3% of the home’s value per year, leaning toward the high end if the house is older.

“Keep it in a separate account so it doesn't get spent on something else. Then treat your inspection report like a calendar. Write down how old the roof, HVAC, and water heater are, and start saving for each one before it fails,” he advises. “I've replaced roofs on both houses I've lived in, and that's not a bill you want to see for the first time.”

Experts believe that manufactured housing is an alternative way to build cheaper homes. However, as a long stigmatized product, financial ease and modernization are necessary.

Earlier this year, the bipartisan Housing for the 21st Century Act was passed, which, among other things, removes the requirement for all manufactured housing to be built on a permanent chassis.

Being built without a chassis cuts down the cost of a unit by roughly $10,000.
